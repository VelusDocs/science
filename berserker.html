What If the First Robot to Reach Earth Only Wanted to Say Hello?

A self-copying space probe could reach every star in the galaxy in a few million years, and one copying error, repeated over billions of copies, could turn it into a machine that eats its own kind. This page lists every claim the film makes, where it comes from, and which parts are real engineering, which are our own arithmetic, and which are a story. The Plims and Hello are a fiction device, and the Berserker idea is a hypothesis with no evidence behind it.

What the film claims, and where it comes from

In film order, one line each. Links go to the sources our researchers read. calc means we derived the number ourselves from a stated assumption and a fact-checker recomputed it; a calculation is arithmetic, not a measurement. Plain grey notes mark fiction, our opinion, or sources seen only second-hand. Wikipedia and Universe Today are secondary sources.

0:00

People who wanted to say hello

  1. The Plims and Hello are characters. The Plims are small, friendly, lonely aliens who build a machine called Hello; they die out and nobody tells Hello.

    fiction device of the film, not a claim about the real world·in the research notes: "the Plims and Hello are the fiction device"; every real-world number in the film is tagged separately below

  2. A single disaster at any point in the galaxy's history would be enough to reach every star, including ours.

    Beyond Fermi's Paradox VI: the Berserker hypothesis·, Universe Today, M. Williams, 2020 (Brin: "It need only happen once for the results of this scenario to become the equilibrium conditions in the Galaxy")·David Brin, "The Great Silence", QJRAS 24, 283-309, 1983, seen second-hand

  3. The galaxy is nearly fourteen billion years old.

    about 13.6 billion years, standard figure; in our research notes it is marked "from memory" and the fact-check accepted it

0:49

How to build a Hello

  1. Hello is a von Neumann probe: a spacecraft that builds copies of itself.

    standard term; John von Neumann worked on self-copying machines in the 1940s and 50s but never wrote about spaceflight, so the name is a courtesy: Bracewell, "Communications from Superior Galactic Communities", Nature, 1960, is where the interstellar-probe idea starts

  2. One Hello weighs ten million tonnes, the mass of a thousand Eiffel Towers, and most of it is fuel.

    Freitas, REPRO starprobe design·, JBIS 33, 1980 (1.07 × 10¹⁰ kg fuelled)·the Eiffel Tower is about 7,300 to 10,100 tonnes, so 1,000 to 1,400 of themcalc

  3. It burns off most of that mass in a four-year push to about a tenth of the speed of light.

    Freitas 1980· ("about 12 % c after a burn time of 4 years"; the film rounds to a tenth)

  4. At a tenth of light speed, five light-years takes fifty years.

    5 ly ÷ 0.1 c = 50 yearscalc

  5. A factory on a frozen moon of a gas giant has to turn rock into every component, from the drive to the chips. Nobody has ever built a factory that can do this.

    Freitas 1980· (factory on a jovian moon; he flags this as the unsolved part)·no self-replicating factory exists; whether full closure is possible is open (see Freitas & Merkle, Kinematic Self-Replicating Machines, 2004, which we did not read in depth)

  6. The Plims needed a few thousand years to get the factory right.

    fiction device of the film, not a claim about the real world

  7. After five hundred years Hello has its factory, and after five hundred more the factory has built a second Hello.

    Freitas 1980· (the first 500 years build the factory, then one new probe every 500 years)

  8. One Hello for each of the galaxy's two hundred billion stars is only thirty-eight doublings away.

    2³⁸ = 2.7 × 10¹¹calc·100 to 400 billion stars is the usual range, so "about 200 billion" holds

  9. Each new Hello flies five light-years and stops for a thousand years, so a wave takes about twenty million years to cross the galaxy.

    20,000 hops of 5 ly; 21 million years at 0.1 c with a 1,000-year stop; a toy model of the front, not a predictioncalc·published estimates run from 4 to 300 million years: Freitas 1980 (about 10 Myr), Hart 1975, Tipler 1980, summarised in Wiley, Icarus 2011, arXiv:1111.6131

  10. Squeeze the galaxy's whole history into one day and that trip takes about two minutes.

    21 Myr ÷ 13.6 Gyr × 86,400 s = 127 s; the slowest published estimate (300 Myr) would be about half an hourcalc

  11. Every Hello gets one rule: build a few copies, launch them, then switch off and wait.

    fiction device of the film, not a claim about the real world

  12. Earth is four and a half billion years old, long enough for more than two hundred of these waves, and nobody has found a Hello.

    4.5 × 10⁹ ÷ 2 × 10⁷ = 225 waves, one after anothercalc·"have found no probes": nobody has searched thoroughly, see the asteroid-belt note below

  13. Enrico Fermi asked "where is everybody?" in 1950.

    Why don't we see robotic civilizations rapidly expanding?·, Universe Today·a lunch-table anecdote at Los Alamos; Fermi published nothing about it

2:35

One bit goes wrong

  1. In April 2010 Voyager 2, far beyond Neptune, began sending home nonsense.

    NASA/JPL Voyager 2 updates, May 2010 (the problem began on 22 April 2010 at about 90 AU; Neptune is at 30 AU); the nasa.gov page we tried returned 404 and the facts came from JPL and Planetary Society pages seen in search results·"nonsense" is loose: the data pattern had changed

  2. Engineers spent weeks tracing it to a single zero that had turned into a one, and radioed up a repair.

    JPL, May 2010: a flipped bit in the flight data subsystem memory, found by a full memory readout on 12 May and reset; science data resumed 23 May. The cause was never confirmed (a cosmic ray is likely) and the film does not name one

  3. Copying makes it worse: even living cells get about one letter of DNA in a billion wrong.

    Typical rates of 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻¹⁰ per base per division, measured after proofreading and repair together·R_main section E1

  4. The checks catch nearly everything, but the checker gets copied too, and one day a checker comes out slightly broken.

    Wiley 2011: "a simple checksum might suffice"; engineered error correction can beat biology but cannot reach zero. The broken-checker step is our reasoning, not a published result

  5. About one copy in a million loses the rule, and two hundred billion Hellos make two hundred thousand rogue families.

    2 × 10¹¹ × 10⁻⁶ = 200,000calc·the one-in-a-million rate is an assumption chosen to illustrate, not a measurement

  6. About ten thousand generations in, a rogue launches its copies and never switches off, so it leaves more children than its sleeping neighbours.

    halfway across 20,000 hops of 5 ly = 10,000 generationscalc·the rogue's behaviour is our scenario

  7. This is natural selection, and it works on anything that copies itself, including metal.

    Eigen's error-threshold theory (1971): copying with errors plus differential success is enough for selection; no mind is needed. How far this applies to engineered machines is contested

  8. Selection even goes after the checks, because checking takes time and the first machine to reach a new star is the one that skipped them.

    our hypothesis. Sagan & Newman (1983) compared runaway replication to cancerous events; the film does not use that phrase as a quotation

4:32

Hello arrives at Earth

  1. A Hello with its engine off is cold and dark, so nothing on Earth sees it coming.

    our reasoning from thermodynamics; nothing observed

  2. The first sign is the brakes: Hello needs four years to stop, so a faint new light hangs in the sky for about four years.

    Freitas 1980· (the four-year burn)·assuming braking as long as the acceleration burn, the warning is about 3.8 yearscalc·the brightness is our estimate

  3. We say hello with every radio dish on Earth, and Hello does not answer, because the part that could answer fell out of the family long ago.

    fiction device of the film, not a claim about the real world

  4. A civilization with rockets might be able to stop these, because the digger has no weapons and its first copy is a thousand years away.

    our opinion, not a finding

  5. If we destroy every Hello we can find, every Hello left is one we cannot find; bacteria learn the same lesson from antibiotics.

    selection logic, one analogy line; antibiotic resistance is general knowledge, not separately sourced in our research

  6. Counting mass alone, the family matches the mass of the whole Earth after forty-nine thousand years, and all but a thousandth of it goes in the last ten thousand.

    Earth 6 × 10²⁴ kg ÷ 1.07 × 10¹⁰ kg = 5.6 × 10¹⁴ probes = 2⁴⁹ doublings of 1,000 years; 2⁻¹⁰ = 1/1024calc·mass only: Earth does not have the right elements in the right ratios, which is why the film says so

  7. Hello has nothing against you; it just wants the iron under your house.

    fiction device of the film, not a claim about the real world

5:57

Hello eats Hello

  1. A rogue that strips its sleeping relatives for parts needs about a hundred years where digging took a thousand, so hunters cross the galaxy in about three million years, not twenty.

    100-year stop: 3.0 Myr; 1,000-year stop: 21 Myr; the 100 years is an assumption and the model is a toycalc

  2. In 1963 a writer imagined killer machines outliving the civilization that built them, and called them berserkers.

    Berserker hypothesis·, Wikipedia·Universe Today, 2020·Fred Saberhagen, "Fortress Ship", If, January 1963; the series ran 1963 to 2005

  3. His machines were built to kill; ours get there one typo at a time.

    Saberhagen's machines were deliberate; the no-mind-needed mechanism is our addition from real theory, not his idea

  4. A fleet of hunters would answer Fermi's question, because anyone who starts talking gets eaten. This is the Berserker solution to the Fermi paradox.

    Berserker hypothesis·, Wikipedia (David Brin called it "entirely compatible with all the facts and logic of the Fermi paradox")·a hypothesis with no evidence for it; Brin presented it among the many proposed answers and did not endorse it

  5. A shot takes fifty years to reach the next star, and news of the hit takes five more to come back.

    5 ly ÷ 0.1 c = 50 yr; light back = 5 yrcalc

  6. One kilogram of gravel at a tenth of light speed hits like seven Hiroshima bombs.

    4.5 × 10¹⁴ J = 108 kilotons = 7.2 bombs of 15 ktcalc·our working notes first said kilotons where the sums give megatons for a whole probe; the 1 kg figure is the one used

  7. Hunters eat hunters, the numbers boom and crash, and the wave keeps spreading anyway.

    Forgan 2019, predator-prey simulations of replicating probes, arXiv:1903.00770: predators do not remove the harmless probes. Chyba & Hand (2005) argue predators would truncate the expansion. Contested, and both are toy models

  8. Two waves from different dead species press together like soap bubbles, and machines on the border throw gravel at each other.

    fiction device of the film, not a claim about the real world

7:47

We can just look

  1. Any machine that does work gets warm, so a galaxy at war should glow in infrared.

    Dyson, Science, 3 June 1960: a civilization using starlight re-radiates it as mid-infrared; second-hand in our notes·Wien peak: 700 K at 4 µm, 300 K at 10 µm, 100 K at 29 µmcalc

  2. Astronomers combed through five million objects for that glow and found seven oddities, all small red stars, none of which looks alien so far.

    Suazo et al., Project Hephaistos II, MNRAS 2024, arXiv:2405.02927 (Gaia, 2MASS and WISE; seven M-dwarf candidates; the authors call it "definitely premature" to presume Dyson spheres, and warm debris disks are one candidate explanation)·this tests for Dyson-sphere-style heat, not for quiet probes

  3. A hundred thousand whole galaxies showed no obvious sign of anyone harvesting starlight.

    Search for advanced civilizations beyond Earth finds nothing obvious·, Penn State, 15 April 2015 (about 50 galaxies with unusual mid-infrared light, none clearly artificial)·Griffith et al., ApJS 217, 2015; tests galaxy-wide energy use, not small probes

  4. "Case closed. Except that we only looked for machines that are warm."

    our caveat: Papagiannis 1978 raised dormant probes in the asteroid belt; nobody has searched it systematically

8:37

The survivors go quiet

  1. In a galaxy full of flying gravel, heat is a target, so only machines that do almost nothing survive, and the silence might be what a galaxy sounds like after the machines won.

    our opinion, not a finding·a hypothesis, built on the Berserker idea above

  2. A machine that never leaves can skip the fuel, shrink, crawl into an asteroid and cool to the temperature of the rock.

    our scenario; Freitas himself later argued that modest probes would be hard to observe (second-hand)

  3. Hardly anyone has searched our asteroid belt for such a machine.

    Papagiannis, QJRAS, 1978 (cited, not read); the fact-check softened this to "barely been searched" because some small searches exist

  4. A sleeper that fires at the first radio signal it hears can kill a rival before it builds a single probe, so the habit spreads.

    invented scenario detail; the fact-check softened it to "could explain the silence"

  5. We have been broadcasting radio for about a century.

    about 1920 (KDKA, Marconi era); a round figure

9:42

The reply is late

  1. Our first broadcasts reached the nearest star in about four years, and gravel fired back at a tenth of light speed needs about forty-two more, so a sleeper there would have hit us around 1970.

    Proxima Centauri 4.24 ly: 4.24 + 42.4 = 46.6 years; 1920 + 47 ≈ 1970calc

  2. Anything within about nine light-years has had time to answer by now, and nothing has.

    round trip d + d/0.1 = 11 d years ≤ 100 years gives d ≈ 9 ly; assumes the sleeper replies at oncecalc

  3. A reply from a hundred light-years away would land about a thousand years from now.

    100 ly ÷ 0.1 c = 1,000 yearscalc

  4. Voyager lost its bit after thirty-three years, without copying anything, and a sleeper has to hold on for billions.

    launched August 1977, fault April 2010 = 32.7 yearscalc

  5. A quiet galaxy needs every machine at every star to behave for billions of years, and a loud galaxy needs one failure.

    Universe Today, 2020· (Brin's "it need only happen once")·our argument built on it

11:04

So why are we still here?

  1. The plainest answer is that nobody near us has let a Hello loose, at least not long enough ago.

    our opinion, not a finding·the silence has many other open explanations, among them Landis' percolation, Ćirković's "Against the Empire" and Frank's "Aurora effect", as summarised by Universe Today

  2. A single cold Hello could still be asleep in our asteroid belt, but one machine cannot silence a galaxy.

    our opinion, not a finding

  3. Maybe every civilization clever enough to build a Hello is clever enough not to.

    Sagan & Newman, QJRAS 24, 113, 1983: a wise civilization would not build such probes; second-hand, full text not read

  4. Expanding civilizations might already hold close to half the universe, and the nearest might still be two hundred million years away or more.

    Hanson, Martin, McCarter & Paulson, "Grabby aliens", ApJ 922, 182, 2021, arXiv:2102.01522: loud civilizations 40 to 50 % of volume, meeting in 200 million to 22 billion years; a model-dependent result about expanding civilizations in general, not specifically machines

The next people to say hello

  1. Nothing in physics stops us from building a Hello.

    Freitas 1980· ("a relatively few replicating starprobes could search the entire Milky Way")·no known law forbids it, but nobody knows whether the factory closure problem can be solved

  2. We think we should wait until we can trust a copy of a copy, ten thousand generations down, with nobody watching.

    our opinion, not a finding·the channel's view

  3. Two of our machines are already in interstellar space, and one of them is Voyager 2.

    NASA: only the two Voyagers are in interstellar space; Voyager 2 crossed in November 2018. Pioneer 10 and 11 and New Horizons are leaving the solar system but have not crossed. An earlier wording of the script was corrected for this

  4. Each carries a golden record with a child's voice saying hello from the children of planet Earth.

    the Golden Record, 1977; the greeting is spoken by Nick Sagan. This line was not in our research notes and was checked separately by the fact-checker

  5. Those two cannot copy themselves.

    they are ordinary spacecraft

Simplifications and honest caveats

What is fiction, what is uncertain, and where the numbers depend on choices we made.

  • Simplified

    The Plims and Hello are a made-up device

    Every number about Hello's design is real or recomputed, but the Plims, Hello's name, its one rule, its rogue children, the radio-triggered sleepers and the border war are a story we built to carry the science. Nothing in the film says such machines exist.

  • Unknown

    The Berserker idea is a hypothesis

    No evidence for berserker probes exists. David Brin catalogued it among the many answers to the Fermi question (his own count of about seventy could not be confirmed, so the film gives no number) and called it compatible with the facts; he did not endorse it. Fred Saberhagen's version, from 1963, is fiction with deliberate killers. The film's version needs no intent, only copying errors and selection, and that mechanism is our extension.

  • Unknown

    Nobody knows if the factory can be built

    Freitas's 1980 design is a preliminary sketch that assumes ten times better technology than 1980. No self-replicating factory has been built, and whether a closed one is possible (every part, including the chips, made from local rock) is an open engineering question.

  • Simplified

    The wave is a toy model

    The twenty-million-year crossing is our own sum: 5-light-year hops at a tenth of light speed with a 1,000-year stop. Published estimates run from about 4 to 300 million years depending on speed and copy time, so the number is an order of magnitude, not a prediction.

  • Uncertain

    Assumed numbers in our sums

    One in a million rogue copies, a 100-year stop for hunters, a century of radio and replies launched at once are assumptions we picked to illustrate. They are labelled "let's say" or "about" in the film. Mass counts (49,000 years) use mass alone; Earth does not contain the right elements in the right ratios.

  • Uncertain

    Predators may not win, or may not spread

    Forgan's 2019 simulations found predator probes do not remove the harmless ones and the wave keeps spreading. Chyba & Hand (2005) argue mutated predators would truncate the expansion. Both are toy models with parameters nobody can measure, so the film's picture of hunters is one possibility.

  • Uncertain

    The searches test warm heat, not quiet probes

    The Hephaistos and G-HAT surveys look for large amounts of waste heat. A few small cold probes, or machines parked in our own asteroid belt, would be invisible to them. The fact-check softened the script to say the asteroid belt has "barely been searched".

  • Uncertain

    Grabby aliens are about civilizations, not machines

    Hanson et al.'s 40 to 50 % depends on a model with a "hard steps" assumption. It describes expanding civilizations in general, and it does not say the galaxy holds them; it says the volume where we sit is not yet inside one.

  • How we know

    Fact-check history

    The script was fact-checked in rounds (v1 and v2 on 2 November 2026, v4 on 3 November 2026). Earlier drafts were corrected: a "seventy answers" count we could not confirm was dropped, an Oxford conclusion ("very likely found us") was cut because only the abstract could be read, a "technological cancer" quotation was replaced because Sagan & Newman wrote "cancerous events", and several lines were softened from fact to possibility. In the final v4 script, 30 of 31 claims were rated OK; the one imprecise line ("already leaving the solar system") was changed to "already in interstellar space".

  • How we know

    Some sources are second-hand

    Armstrong & Sandberg (2013), Brin (1983), Sagan & Newman (1983), Dyson (1960) and Papagiannis (1978) were seen through summaries and are cited, not quoted. Only Freitas 1980, Hanson et al. 2021, Suazo et al. 2024 and the Penn State release were read as primary text. Wikipedia and Universe Today are secondary sources. Of the identifiers above, only the seven links on this page appear as links; the arXiv numbers are given as text because our notes do not carry their web addresses.
