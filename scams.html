Why Smart People Fall for Scams

A heart surgeon gets a call from her bank. Intelligence turns out to be a poor shield, because a con does not argue with the careful part of your mind: it goes for the alarms, and then it starts a clock. This page lists every factual line in the film, with its source, and says plainly what is a reported number, an estimate, a model or a drawing.

What the film claims, and where it comes from

In film order, one or two sentences per line, in the words of the narration. calc means we derived the number ourselves and re-ran the arithmetic. The theatre, the control room and its levers are metaphors; Ellen and her call are invented; the hidden counter in the corner is $504 per second of film, from the FTC's reported losses. 0:00

Hook

  1. This is Ellen, a heart surgeon. Her phone is ringing: it's her bank's fraud team, someone is draining her account, and she has to move her money within the hour.

    Ellen and the call are invented, as the film says. The script imitates a common bank-imposter call; the theatre is a metaphor

  2. Scams like hers drew more than a million reports in America last year.

    FTC testimony to the Joint Economic Committee, 25 March 2026 ("over 1 million reports about imposter frauds" in 2025, with over $3.5 billion reported lost)

  3. Last year, Americans reported losing fifteen point nine billion dollars to scams.

    FTC testimony to the Joint Economic Committee ("consumers submitted 3 million fraud reports to CSN and reported $15.9 billion in losses"). The word "reported" matters; see the notes

  1. That's about five hundred dollars every second, day and night.

    $15.9 billion divided by 31,536,000 seconds in a year is $504 a secondcalc·Average over a year, from reported losses

  2. Each of those dollars left in a small private play, staged for an audience of one.

    Metaphor, not a claim

1:13

The worst play ever written

  1. A prince, a fortune frozen in a foreign bank, a small fee to set it free. Who would fall for that? Most people wouldn't.

    Herley, Microsoft Research, 2012 ("Far-fetched tales of West African riches strike most as comical")

  1. According to one well-known analysis, the badness is in the script: every reply costs the scammer time, and a victim who gets suspicious halfway through wastes the whole performance.

    Herley, 2012 (an attacker with few viable victims has "an over-riding need to reduce false positives")

  2. Anyone with doubts walks out after the first line, and the few who stay are the likeliest to stay until the final act.

    Herley, 2012 (an email "that repels all but the most gullible" lets "the most promising marks" self-select)

  3. No scammer has confessed to this, and it's only a computer scientist's model, but a very tidy one.

    Herley, 2012 (an economic argument, not an experiment and not scammers' testimony; see the notes)

  4. In an older study of investment fraud, the victims scored higher on a money quiz than people who hadn't been fooled.

    NASD Investor Education Foundation, Investor Fraud Study: Final Report, 12 May 2006 ("investment fraud victims scored higher than non-victims on eight financial literacy questions")

  5. That doesn't make knowledge dangerous: people with savings simply get more invitations.

    The film’s gloss, not a tested result. The same report built a subgroup of likely active investors; victims outscored them too, and the report concludes literacy programs are “probably not sufficient”: NASD, 2006

  6. What does turn up as a risk, in some studies, is confidence, the feeling that you know more than you do.

    Xiao, Li & Zhou, Economics Letters, 2022 ("Financial literacy overconfidence and investment fraud victimization"). We confirmed the paper exists but could not read its abstract; evidence is mixed, hence "in some studies"

  7. Nor is this a grandparent problem: younger adults actually report losing money to scams more often than older ones.

    FTC, Protecting Older Consumers 2024-2025, 1 December 2025 (older adults "reported losing money to fraud at a lower rate than younger adults")

  1. Older adults lose less often, but bigger, with a typical loss of nine hundred dollars over sixty and more than sixteen hundred over eighty.

    FTC, Protecting Older Consumers 2024-2025 (2024 median reported loss: $900 for ages 60 and over; $1,650 for ages 80 and over)

  2. A few disasters do most of the damage: the six-figure losses, just one report in twenty, held two thirds of all the money older adults lost.

    FTC, Protecting Older Consumers 2024-2025 (six-figure losses were "5% of older adults' loss reports in 2024" but "68% of their aggregate reported losses")

  3. In a study of fraud victims, fewer than one in twenty complained to an agency or a watchdog.

    Anderson, "To Whom Do Victims of Mass-Market Consumer Fraud Complain?", SSRN, 2021 (4.8% complained to a Better Business Bureau or a government entity), as cited in FTC testimony to the Joint Economic Committee, footnote 6. SSRN blocks bots; see the notes

  4. By the regulator's own upper estimate, the true cost could be more than fifteen times what gets reported.

    FTC testimony to the Joint Economic Committee (the FTC's estimate of the overall cost of fraud "could be as high as $195.9 billion" for 2024)·FTC press release, March 2025 ($12.5 billion reported for 2024)·195.9 divided by 12.5 is 15.7calc

3:26

Inside the control room

  1. Let's picture Ellen's brain as a control room: a calm operator checks every claim against what she knows, but the room also has alarms, wired to fear, desire and pain.

    Loewenstein, "Out of Control: Visceral Influences on Behavior", Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes 65(3), 1996 (visceral factors such as emotions, drives and pain). The control room is our picture, not Loewenstein's

  2. When they go off, they take over the room in a sort of emotional coup, a hot state. In a hot state, people act against their own interests, sometimes while knowing perfectly well that they are doing it.

    Loewenstein, 1996 (people "act against their self-interest in full knowledge that they are doing so"). We could not open the abstract; the quote comes from our research notes. "Emotional coup" is the film's metaphor

  1. Fear: your account is being emptied. Love: your grandson is in trouble. Greed: a million dollars, or a never-ending vacation.

    Illustrative, not a counted list. The grandson call is the FTC's own example: FTC Consumer Alert, 20 March 2023

  2. That last lever is the phantom, a prize so dazzling it fills every screen in the room.

    Pratkanis & Shadel, Weapons of Fraud: A Source Book for Fraud Fighters, AARP, 2005 (a "phantom" such as "a million dollars, or a never-ending vacation"). The book is not online; we confirmed it exists, not the quote

  3. On hundreds of undercover recordings of real scam pitches, the same moves turn up again and again. The caller plays a role: an authority, a friend, sometimes even someone who depends on you.

    Pratkanis & Shadel, Weapons of Fraud, 2005 (analysis of undercover tapes of telemarketing cons). Sources disagree on the tape count, so the film says "hundreds"

  4. Psychologists have catalogued these levers for decades: a uniform, a deadline, a crowd that's already in, a favour you feel you owe. They're a vocabulary, not laws of nature.

    Pratkanis & Shadel, Weapons of Fraud, 2005. The "vocabulary, not laws" hedge is ours: replication of classic influence studies is uneven

  5. In a lab game, rigged to make players excited or angry, older adults then said they'd be likelier to buy from misleading adverts.

    Kircanski et al., Heightened Emotional States Increase Susceptibility to Fraud in Older Adults, Stanford Center on Longevity / FINRA Investor Education Foundation (139 people, 71 aged 65 to 85; excited and angry groups "reported greater intention to purchase")

  6. Younger players showed no clear change, at least in that small test.

    Kircanski et al. (the younger group, aged 30 to 40, showed no significant group differences). Stated intention in a lab, not real purchases

  7. Falling for a con, the people who measure persuasion argue, isn't directly tied to intelligence, but to moments when reasoning gets switched off.

    Modic, Anderson & Palomäki, PLoS One, 2018 ("vulnerability to persuasion is not directly linked to intelligence, but to mechanisms that momentarily suspend the rational decision-making processes", a view the authors credit to earlier work by Fischer and Lea)

  8. That may be why there's so often a clock in the script: within the hour, before the offer ends, before your grandson spends a night in a cell.

    The film’s inference, flagged with “may be”. The FTC’s own grandson example is: FTC Consumer Alert

  9. Every control room has a pause lever, and the clock is there to keep your hand off it.

    Metaphor, not a claim

5:43

The show goes industrial

  1. Bernie Madoff's audience was no crowd of gullible strangers. It included hedge funds, pension funds and charities, as well as private investors.

    We found this in search results quoting a Justice Department filing, but did not open the filing itself. The court record we did open concerns the statements, not the mix of investors

  2. Their account statements added up to about sixty-five billion dollars. The statements were props.

    Second Circuit, In re Bernard L. Madoff Investment Securities LLC, 16 August 2011 (final customer statements "falsely recorded nearly $64.8 billion of net investments and related fictitious gains")·Associated Press / NBC News, 1 March 2010 ("$64.8 billion reflected on fictitious statements")

  1. A judge later said they reflected Madoff's fantasy world of trading activity.

    Judge Burton Lifland, bankruptcy court, 1 March 2010, as quoted in Ritholtz, The Big Picture ("bogus and reflected Madoff's fantasy world of trading activity"). We did not open the opinion itself; see the notes

  2. The money actually lost, cash in minus cash out, was about twenty billion dollars. Less than a third of the paper fortune, but every dollar of it real.

    Ritholtz, The Big Picture (about $20 billion of cash invested; account balances of nearly $65 billion)·20 divided by 64.8 is 31%calc

  3. Say a text arrives from a stranger, sorry, wrong number, and when you reply politely, the stranger turns out to be delightful.

    Oak & Shafiq, "Hello, is this Anna?": Unpacking the Lifecycle of Pig-Butchering Scams, USENIX SOUPS 2025 (26 victim interviews; many first contacts were messages pretending to be misdirected)

  4. The first act is the hunt, the second act, the raise, lasts weeks, and on the app your money grows beautifully and a small test withdrawal works. The final act begins when you try to take the big money out: fees, then more fees, then silence.

    Oak & Shafiq, 2025 (platforms with "fabricated investment returns"; a few victims were allowed small early withdrawals; large withdrawals met demands for "withdrawal fees or taxes"). The act names (hunt, raise, kill) come from practitioner explainers, not from a controlled study

  5. The play's Chinese name translates as pig butchering. Fatten first, slaughter later.

    Common English rendering of sha zhu pan, the term used in the research above; we did not find a primary linguistic source

  6. Investment scams, the family this play belongs to, now account for about half of all the money Americans report losing.

    FTC testimony to the Joint Economic Committee (investment scams: over $7.9 billion; "approximately half of the total reported fraud losses for 2025")

  7. Measured in dollars, the top way in isn't email or a phone call, but social media.

    FTC testimony to the Joint Economic Committee ("Social media, however, is the top contact method by aggregate reported losses": over $2 billion in FY 2025)

  8. In twenty twenty-four, a finance worker in Hong Kong joined a video call with the company's finance chief and several colleagues. They looked right, they sounded right, and each of them was a deepfake.

    CNN Business, 16 May 2024 (Hong Kong police: a finance worker was duped into a video call with people believed to be the chief financial officer and other staff, all deepfake re-creations; the firm was later named as Arup)

  1. According to police, the worker had been suspicious of the request at first, until the faces on the call put the doubts to rest.

    CNN Business ("the worker had initially suspected he had received a phishing email"; he "put aside his doubts after the video call"). We did not guess the worker's gender

  2. Transfer after transfer, about twenty-five million dollars left the firm.

    CNN Business (HK$200 million, "about $25.6 million", across 15 transactions, as reported by RTHK citing police). We reached RTHK only through CNN's report

  3. Some are typed in scam compounds in Southeast Asia, by people lured there with fake job offers.

    UN News, 29 August 2023, reporting the UN human rights office (OHCHR) report (gangs lure recruits with job offers that appear legitimate)·Al Jazeera, 29 August 2023

  4. In Myanmar and Cambodia alone, in twenty twenty-three the U.N. human rights office estimated that around two hundred and twenty thousand people may be trapped inside.

    UN News ("at least 120,000" people in Myanmar and 100,000 in Cambodia "may be held")·Al Jazeera·120,000 + 100,000 = 220,000calc

  1. That's enough to fill about three big football stadiums.

    220,000 divided by a 75,000-seat stadium is 2.9; the stadium size is our choicecalc

  2. In the words of the U.N.'s human rights chief, they are victims. They are not criminals. So in some cases, there's a victim at each end of the chat.

    UN News (Volker Türk: "They are victims. They are not criminals."; the report says rescued victims are often penalised rather than protected). "At each end of the chat" is the film's inference, hence "in some cases"

How to walk out of a play

  1. In interviews with a small group of romance-scam victims, most said losing the relationship hurt more than losing the money.

    Whitty & Buchanan, Criminology & Criminal Justice, 2016 ("For most, the loss of the relationship was more upsetting than their financial losses"). The abstract does not give the sample size

  2. Being conned is not an intelligence test you failed. It's a play written, rehearsed and staged for someone exactly like you.

    Framing built on Modic et al., 2018 (see above). The paper does not compare victims with non-victims on intelligence

  3. So if being smart doesn't protect you, what does? Time, and a second person.

    The film’s conclusion, not a study result. It rests on the forewarning and call-back items below

  4. The most useful habit is pulling the pause lever: hang up, and call back on a number you already know. A cloned voice needs only a short audio clip, but it can't answer a phone it doesn't own.

    FTC Consumer Alert, 20 March 2023 ("All he needs is a short audio clip of your family member's voice"; "Use a phone number you know is theirs")

  1. We found no controlled trial of this rule, but it aims at the thing every script needs, your next few minutes.

    An absence claim: our search found no controlled trial of the call-back rule. The reasoning is ours

  2. When volunteers warned past fraud victims about scams, those victims were later better at turning down a fake scam call.

    Stanford Center on Longevity, on a study in Basic and Applied Social Psychology (prior telemarketing-fraud victims were warned by AARP Fraud Fighter volunteers, then called with a mock scam pitch; forewarning reduced acceptance)

  3. But the spoiler wore off. A warning about the exact same scam still helped after two weeks, and was gone by four.

    Stanford Center on Longevity (the same-scam warning "was only effective after the 2-week delay"; a warning about a different scam was still effective after four weeks, which the film does not claim)

  4. Against fake news, short spoiler videos on YouTube made viewers slightly better at spotting manipulation tricks.

    Roozenbeek et al., "Psychological inoculation improves resilience against misinformation on social media", Science Advances, 2022 (five short videos; a field study on YouTube, n = 22,632, improved recognition of manipulation techniques). About misinformation, not scams. Science.org blocks bots; see the notes

  5. We found no measurement of whether that cuts real scam losses.

    An absence claim from our search, not a finding

  6. This video, too, has a script. Like any warning, its effect will probably wear off within weeks.

    Inferred from the decay seen in the Stanford forewarning study; hedged in the film with "probably"

  7. At last year's rate, more than three hundred thousand dollars was reported lost to scams while you watched it.

    $504 a second times about 670 seconds of film is about $338,000; the film’s hidden counter ends at 338,184 near 11:11calc·Rate from FTC testimony to the Joint Economic Committee, reported losses only

  8. Ellen's phone is ringing again. This time she says she'll call back, hangs up, and dials the number printed on her card. Every con is a play for an audience of one. Make it two.

    Invented ending, a dramatisation of the call-back habit above

Simplifications and honest caveats

Eleven minutes is short. Here is what we left out, simplified or could not confirm.

  • Uncertain

    The money is "reported", not "lost"

    The $15.9 billion is what people told the FTC they lost (3 million fraud reports in 2025). It is not a count of every loss, and the FTC itself calls it a fraction of the real total. The film says "reported" every time. The per-second figure is a yearly average of that reported total, so "while you watched" is an illustration, not a measurement.

  • Uncertain

    $195.9 billion is an upper estimate

    The FTC says overall fraud cost for 2024 "could be as high as" $195.9 billion, built on the finding that few victims complain. We divided it by the $12.5 billion reported for 2024 to get "more than fifteen times". It is a modelled ceiling for a different year than the $15.9 billion, not a count.

  • Uncertain

    The prince email is a model, not a confession

    Herley's argument is an economic model of how a scammer manages false positives. It is not an experiment and no scammer has said this publicly. The film says "according to one well-known analysis", "may be on purpose" and "No scammer has confessed".

  • Uncertain

    Smart people: thin evidence on both sides

    The money-quiz result is from one 2006 study of investment fraud and a self-selected sample, and the "people with savings get more invitations" explanation is the film's own, untested. Confidence as a risk factor shows up in some studies and not others, and we could not read the abstract of the one we cite. The film never says smart people are easier to fool; it says intelligence is a poor shield.

  • Uncertain

    220,000 is a UN estimate of people who may be trapped

    The U.N. human rights office wrote "at least 120,000" in Myanmar and about 100,000 in Cambodia "may be held" in scam operations, from credible sources, in a clandestine setting. The film adds the two and says "may be trapped" and "around". The three stadiums are our comparison at 75,000 seats each; stadiums vary.

  • Simplified

    The lab game is a small test

    139 people played a rigged money game, then rated misleading adverts. The older groups said they would be likelier to buy; the younger group showed no clear change. It measures stated intention in a lab, not real purchases, which is why the film says "at least in that small test".

  • Uncertain

    No controlled trial of the call-back rule

    The FTC advises hanging up and calling a number you know, and the logic fits the findings on time pressure. We found no controlled trial showing it cuts losses, and the film says so. The same goes for spoiler videos: the YouTube study measured recognition of manipulation techniques in misinformation, not scam losses.

  • Simplified

    The romance study is a small interview group

    Whitty and Buchanan interviewed a small group of victims. "Most said the relationship hurt more" describes that group, not all romance-scam victims. The abstract does not state the sample size, and our research notes disagree about it, so the page does not give a number.

  • Simplified

    Forewarning fades, in one study

    The Stanford page does not give a sample size or percentages. It says the same-scam warning worked after two weeks and not after four, while a warning about a different scam still worked at four weeks. The film uses only the first result, and it applies the same fade to its own message with "probably".

  • Uncertain

    Madoff: which twenty billion, and which judge

    The $64.8 billion on the statements is in the Second Circuit's opinion. The "about $20 billion" is the cash customers put in, from a secondary article; the trustee's net-loss figure is not the same number and other accounts give lower principal figures. The "fantasy world of trading activity" quote is attributed to Judge Lifland (1 March 2010) through a news summary; we did not open the opinion itself. The mix of hedge funds, pension funds and charities rests on search results quoting a Justice Department filing, which we did not open.

  • Simplified

    Pig butchering: the play is a template

    In the 26-victim interview study, only a few victims were allowed a small early withdrawal, and many contacts began with a misdirected text; fee demands at withdrawal were common. "Hunt, raise, kill" are practitioners' labels. The film tells the typical story as one story.

  • Simplified

    The Hong Kong call, from police via the press

    The facts come from Hong Kong police statements as reported by CNN and RTHK, and from the firm's later confirmation. We reached RTHK only through CNN. The film says "according to police" for the worker's early doubts and does not guess the worker's gender.

  • Simplified

    Source access

    SSRN (Anderson), Science.org (Roozenbeek), the SAGE DOI (Whitty and Buchanan) and the Elsevier DOI (Loewenstein) block automated readers. For these we confirmed title, authors, journal and year through OpenAlex or Crossref, and for Whitty and Roozenbeek also the abstract. Anderson's 4.8% is read from the FTC testimony's footnote, not from the paper. Loewenstein's wording is from our research notes. Weapons of Fraud is a printed book; we confirmed it exists and what it covers but could not check the "phantom" quote or the tape count, so the film says "hundreds". The FTC pages open in a browser but sometimes return an error to scripts.

  • Left out

    Stylisation, and what we left out

    The theatre, the control room, the pause lever and the cardboard sets are metaphors; Ellen and her call are invented. Left out: cognitive decline as a separate risk (a different story from "smart people"), voice-clone cases of 2019 and survey figures from security vendors, and the FBI's own loss totals, which count different things.
