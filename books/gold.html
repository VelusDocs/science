Your Gold Did Not Come From Earth

The oldest history of the metal on your finger. Fusion in stars stops at iron, so gold has to be built some other way. This book follows it from the crash of two dead stars to a ring you can hold, and says plainly which parts are still debated.

Added in the book

Facts that are in the book but not in the film, and the places where the book says something different from the film. calc means the number is our own arithmetic.

  1. A gram of neutrons is about six hundred thousand billion billion particles, more than ten thousand times the number of air molecules in the same space.

    R-process, Wikipedia ("almost a gram of free neutrons in every cubic centimeter")·1 g ÷ 1.675 × 10⁻²⁴ g per neutron ≈ 6 × 10²³; air at sea level holds about 2.5 × 10¹⁹ molecules per cm³, a standard value. That gives roughly 24,000 times; the book's fact-check set the wording at "more than ten thousand"calc

  2. Left alone, a free neutron falls apart in about fifteen minutes on average, and half of them are gone in ten.

    standard values checked in the book's fact-check: mean lifetime 878 s, half-life about 610 s; no page linked

  3. Strontium, element 38, was the first heavy element to be identified in the light of a neutron-star merger.

    Watson et al., Nature 574, 497–500 (2019)·AT2017gfo, Wikipedia ("direct proof of strontium production during such an event")

  4. Different from the film: published estimates of the gold made in the 2017 crash run from about ten Earth masses to a hundred or more. The film gives "about 3 to about 100". Debated.

    AT2017gfo, Wikipedia (about 10 Earth masses of gold and platinum)·NASA SVS ("hundreds of Earth masses of gold and platinum")·the film's lower figure of 3 is the Wikipedia range we could not trace to a primary paper; the book leaves it out

  5. About ten Earths' worth of gold would make a ball about 1.42 times as wide as the Earth, which is how the book draws it.

    radius = (10 × 5.51 ÷ 19.3)^(1/3) = 1.42 Earth radii, using the densities of the Earth (5.51 g/cm³) and of gold (19.3 g/cm³)calc

  6. More exact than the film: all the gold ever mined, about 222,600 tonnes, would fit in a cube about 22.6 metres on each side. The film rounds this to 23 metres, and so does the book's headline.

    World Gold Council: how much gold has been mined, World Gold Council

The Cold Open

  1. Gold in a ring is older than the Earth and older than the Sun: the metal was made before the cloud that formed them collapsed.

    Space.com, where-did-gold-come-from, Space.com (search snippet only; the page itself was not opened)·the 4.6 billion year age of the Sun and Earth is standard; no single primary page was fetched

  2. Nothing on Earth could have made that gold, and neither could the Sun: stars fuse only up to the iron group, and gold needs a different process.

    Nuclear binding energy, Wikipedia·R-process, Wikipedia ("approximately half of the atomic nuclei heavier than iron")

  3. A 5 g ring holds about fifteen thousand billion billion (1.5 × 10²²) atoms of gold.

    our calculation: 5 g ÷ 196.97 g/mol × 6.022 × 10²³; the 5 g ring is our assumptioncalc

  4. The same metal is in your phone and maybe in your teeth.

    Gold, Wikipedia (corrosion-resistant electrical connectors in computerised devices; dentistry); we did not link the page·a phone holds only a few hundredths of a gram (soft figure from trade sites, not used on screen as a number)

Why the Sun Can't Make Gold

  1. Stars spend their lives fusing small nuclei into bigger ones, and each fusion releases energy. The biggest stars keep going up to iron.

    Nuclear binding energy, Wikipedia ("For elements that weigh more than iron… the fusion process no longer releases energy"); the page does not itself say "stars stop at iron", which is standard stellar physics, so the film says iron and nickel, the iron group

  2. Iron and nickel have the most tightly bound nuclei there are, so fusing them into anything heavier costs energy instead of releasing it.

    Nuclear binding energy, Wikipedia ("nickel-62 is the most tightly bound nucleus"; iron-56 "the most efficiently bound")

  3. An iron nucleus holds 56 particles and a gold nucleus 197.

    Nuclear binding energy, Wikipedia (iron-56)·R-process, Wikipedia (gold, A = 197, mainly an r-process element)

  4. So fusion can never make gold, however big the star.

    Nuclear binding energy, Wikipedia·R-process, Wikipedia

  5. Iron is “the ash of a star”: you cannot keep a fire going by burning the ash.

    our analogy, not a sourced claim; the film uses it once, after showing the real physics

The Recipe for Gold

  1. Gold is built with neutrons, by the r-process (rapid neutron capture), which makes about half of the nuclei heavier than iron; the slow s-process makes the rest.

    R-process, Wikipedia

  2. A neutron has no electric charge, so a nucleus does not push it away and it can stick. When a nucleus holds too many neutrons, some turn into protons, and each new proton moves the atom up one element until it reaches gold.

    textbook nuclear physics (beta decay), checked as standard in our fact-check; not tied to a page we opened

  3. The overstuffed nuclei fall apart quickly, so the next neutrons must arrive first: up to about 100 captures per second.

    R-process, Wikipedia ("up to 100 captures per second")

  4. The process needs about a gram of free neutrons in every cubic centimetre, the size of a sugar cube (about 10²⁴ per cm³).

    R-process, Wikipedia ("almost a gram of free neutrons in every cubic centimeter")

  5. In 1957 four scientists (Burbidge, Burbidge, Fowler and Hoyle) named the r-process and guessed that supernovae were where it happens.

    B2FH paper, Wikipedia (Rev. Mod. Phys. 29, 547)·R-process, Wikipedia (the review "named the r-process and outlined the physics that guides it"; supernovae proposed "probably")

  6. For about sixty years the supernova was the standard answer, but simulations of these explosions kept falling short of neutrons.

    Origin of the heaviest elements: the rapid neutron-capture process, Cowan et al., Rev. Mod. Phys., 2021 ("more recent studies have cast substantial doubts on this environment")·R-process, Wikipedia (supernova ejecta must be neutron-rich, "a condition which has been difficult to achieve in models")·“sixty years”: 1957 to the 2017 merger, our arithmeticcalc

The Day Two Dead Stars Crashed

  1. A neutron star holds about the mass of the Sun in a ball about 20 km across; a sugar cube of it would weigh about a billion tonnes.

    Neutron stars, NASA Imagine the Universe ("about 20 kilometers"; a sugar cube "would weigh about 1 trillion kilograms")·1 trillion kg = 10⁹ tonnescalc

  2. Gravity keeps those neutrons locked inside; something has to rip the star apart, and a second neutron star could do it.

    textbook physics for the first half·Kasen et al., Nature 551, 80 (2017) (abstract: mergers are likely "a dominant mode of r-process production")

  3. On 17 August 2017, at 12:41 universal time, three detectors felt a gravitational wave from two neutron stars that had merged about 130 million years of light travel away, in the galaxy NGC 4993. Measured.

    AT2017gfo, Wikipedia (12:41:04.4 UTC; LIGO Hanford, Livingston and Virgo)·NASA Goddard SVS 12740, NASA Goddard SVS (NGC 4993, about 130 million light-years)

  4. Less than two seconds later, a space telescope caught a flash of gamma rays. Measured.

    AT2017gfo, Wikipedia (Fermi: "1.74±0.05 s after the merger time and lasting for about 2 seconds")·NASA SVS

  5. A telescope in Chile photographed a new point of light in that galaxy less than eleven hours after the ripple. Measured.

    AT2017gfo, Wikipedia (the Swope image, 10 h 52 min after the event; "six teams imaged the same new source independently in a 90-minute interval")

  6. Over the next days the light changed from blue to red as the debris, thrown out at about a tenth of the speed of light, expanded and cooled. Measured.

    AT2017gfo, Wikipedia ("the color of the optical source changed from blue to red as the source expanded and cooled"; about 10 percent of light speed)·0.1 × 299,792 km/s ≈ 30,000 km/scalc

  7. This glow is a kilonova, powered by the radioactive decay of freshly made atoms.

    NASA Goddard SVS 12740, NASA Goddard SVS ("glow powered by decay of newly forged radioactive elements")

  8. The blue light came from a cloud of lighter atoms and the red light from a cloud of much heavier ones, the group gold belongs to.

    Kasen et al., Nature 2017 (light r-process, A < 140, blue; heavy, A > 140, red)

  9. Astronomers read the fingerprints of freshly made elements in that light, first strontium and later a few more. Measured.

    AT2017gfo, Wikipedia ("direct proof of strontium production during such an event"; yttrium, lanthanum and cerium identified later)

  10. The amount of gold was calculated from the size of the red cloud, not measured atom by atom. Estimates for this one crash run from about 3 to about 100 Earth masses of gold; they differ because the ejected mass and the heavy-cloud share depend on the model. Debated.

    AT2017gfo, Wikipedia (about 10 Earth masses of gold and platinum, E. Berger, press conference 16 Oct 2017; ejecta about 16,000 Earth masses of heavy elements)·NASA SVS ("hundreds of Earth masses of gold and platinum")·R-process, Wikipedia ("between 3 and 13 Earth masses of gold", not traced to a primary paper)·Pian et al., Nature 2017 (ejecta 0.03 to 0.05 Suns)·LIGO summary (lower ejecta estimates)

  11. All the gold ever mined would fit in a cube about 23 metres on each side, rings, coins and bars included.

    World Gold Council: how much gold has been mined, World Gold Council (about 222,600 tonnes; cube "around 22.6 metres on each side"; checked in November 2026)·222,600 t ÷ 19.32 t/m³ = 11,522 m³, side 22.6 mcalc

  12. That one crash made more gold than the whole Earth weighs. True at every estimate we found, but the size is a model result.

    NASA SVS·AT2017gfo, Wikipedia·even the lowest figure, 3 Earth masses, is about 8 × 10¹⁶ times all gold ever minedcalc

The Crash Nobody Saw

  1. The Sun and the Earth formed about 4.6 billion years ago from a cloud of gas and dust that already held gold, so the 2017 crash is far too recent to have made your ring.

    Space.com, where-did-gold-come-from, Space.com (search snippet only)·age of the Earth is standard; no single primary page was fetched

  2. In December 2004 a magnetar, a neutron star with an extreme magnetic field, released a giant flare: in a tenth of a second, more energy than the Sun gives off in 150,000 years.

    NASA Swift, 2005, NASA ("150,000 years" in 0.1 s; search snippet)·NASA Imagine the Universe, Feb 2005, NASA·SGR 1806-20, Wikipedia (27 Dec 2004). The discovery paper (Hurley et al., Nature) says 0.2 s and a quarter of a million years; we quote NASA

  3. About ten minutes after the spike, a second, fainter glow of gamma rays rose and faded over hours; nobody could explain it for twenty years.

    Direct evidence for r-process nucleosynthesis in delayed MeV emission from the SGR 1806-20 magnetar giant flare, Patel et al., ApJL 984, L29, 2025 (rises about 10 min after the spike, fades over hours)

  4. In 2025 astronomers found that this glow matches the radioactive decay of freshly made heavy atoms, from crust the flare blew off the star.

    Patel et al., ApJL 2025·Ohio State University news release, Ohio State University

  5. So astronomers had recorded this process thirteen years before the famous crash without knowing it.

    Dec 2004 to Aug 2017 is 12.6 years, rounded to thirteencalc

  6. Flares like this one make at least about 1% to 10% of the heavy atoms in our galaxy. An estimate from a single flare.

    Patel et al., ApJL 2025 (abstract: "at least ~1%-10%", for r-process elements with A ≥ 90, not gold alone; about 10−6 Suns of r-process material in the flare)·Ohio State ("up to 10%")·our fact-check changed an earlier "a few percent" to "one percent"

  7. Nobody knows how much gold comes from crashes, how much from magnetar flares, and how much from a third source, rapidly spinning stars that collapse. Debated.

    Cowan et al., Rev. Mod. Phys. 2021·Kasen et al., 2017 (mergers "a dominant mode")·Columbia University news (node 8205) (Metzger: the second time we have directly seen where these elements form, after mergers; page not fetched, snippet only)·Siegel, Barnes and Metzger, Nature 569, 2019 (collapsars may make over 80%), and Kobayashi et al., ApJL 943, L12, 2023 (mergers alone fail timing tests): read as abstracts, no URL in our files

  8. A gold atom from a crash is identical to one from a flare, so the ring cannot tell us.

    textbook physics: all gold-197 atoms are identical

  9. All of this debris mixed into the clouds that formed new stars and planets, so your ring may hold atoms from many catastrophes.

    Ohio State (T. Thompson: "All of that material they eject gets mixed into the next generation of planets and stars")·Syfy Wire article on a nearby neutron-star collision, Syfy (press coverage of Bartos and Marka, 2019; one merger may account for about 0.3%)

Why You Can Hold Gold at All

  1. The young Earth was hot enough for its heavy iron to sink to the centre and form the core.

    standard geophysics, checked as textbook

  2. Gold mixes easily with iron, so nearly all of the Earth’s gold went down with it, under about 3,000 km of solid rock. More than 99.999% by one estimate.

    University of Göttingen press release no. 85, 22 May 2025 (Messling et al., Nature 2025: "More than 99.999% of Earth’s stores of gold and other precious metals lie buried under 3,000 km of solid rock"). The paper measured ruthenium isotopes; gold is inferred·ScienceAlert·Discover (other outlets say more than 99%)

  3. After the core formed, a rain of meteorites kept hitting the Earth, and the gold they carried stayed near the surface. The favoured explanation, not settled. Debated.

    Willbold, Elliott and Moorbath, Nature 477, 195, 2011 (paywalled; read through its abstract and press coverage)·Brenan and McDonough, Nature Geoscience, 2009, argue core-formation chemistry alone could explain mantle gold; not opened

  4. One clue is in the tungsten of 3.8-billion-year-old rocks, which differs slightly from modern rocks (about 13 parts per million in tungsten-182), as a late meteorite rain would leave it.

    Willbold et al., Nature 2011 (Isua rocks, Greenland)·a later conference abstract (Tusch, Goldschmidt 2017) found Isua mantle rocks that look modern, against a missing veneer

  5. Two thirds of all the gold ever mined was dug out of the ground after 1950.

    World Gold Council: how much gold has been mined, World Gold Council

  6. Catastrophes in our galaxy forged the atoms within seconds, more than four and a half billion years ago; probably crashing neutron stars, maybe flares like the one of 2004.

    R-process, Wikipedia (captures must beat radioactive decay)·Kasen et al., 2017·Patel et al., 2025·no source we opened states how long the neutron-capture burst lasts; "within seconds" is our safe wording, and "probably" is a hedge, see caveats

  7. The atoms ended up in a cloud that collapsed into the Sun and Earth; the Earth swallowed nearly all its gold, and meteorites brought a tiny amount to the surface.

    Space.com (snippet)·Göttingen·Nature 2011

The Ring in Your Hand

  1. The ring is debris, the wreckage of catastrophes nobody saw, and its metal is older than the ground you stand on.

    Space.com, where-did-gold-come-from, Space.com (snippet)·the Earth is about 4.6 billion years old; the gold was made before it

  2. This gold fell out of the sky onto a planet that had already swallowed almost all the rest.

    Göttingen·Willbold et al., 2011. The sky delivery is the favoured view, not unanimous

Simplifications and honest caveats

What was measured, what is still argued over, and where the numbers depend on choices we made. Written for the film, and they apply to the book too.

  • Measured

    One crash, seen in every kind of light

    The gravitational wave, the gamma-ray flash 1.74 seconds later, the new point of light within eleven hours, the blue-to-red change over days and the strontium in the spectrum were all recorded in August 2017. That the glow is powered by freshly made heavy atoms is the accepted reading of those data.

  • Debated

    Who made most of the gold

    Neutron star mergers are the only source seen directly in the act, and a leading candidate. A 2019 estimate gave rapidly spinning, collapsing stars (collapsars) more than 80%; a 2023 study found that mergers alone fail some timing tests against the age of old stars; a 2025 paper adds magnetar flares at roughly 1% to 10%. The film says nobody knows the split, and its "probably crashing neutron stars, maybe flares" is a hedge, not a result. We did not open the Nature papers behind the first two; we read their abstracts.

  • Uncertain

    How much gold one crash made

    Published figures for the 2017 crash run from about 3 Earth masses of gold to about 100, and NASA's own page says "hundreds" for gold and platinum together. The 3 to 13 figure on Wikipedia could not be traced to a primary paper. That is why the film does not give a number and says only "more gold than the whole Earth weighs", which holds at every estimate. The amount is calculated from the size of the red cloud, not measured.

  • Uncertain

    The magnetar share rests on one flare

    The 1% to 10% comes from a single observed flare, and the authors call it uncertain. It is a share of all heavy r-process elements (mass number 90 and up), not of gold alone. Press versions differ ("up to 10%", "10% or more"). Our fact-check caught and fixed an earlier line in the script that said "a few percent".

  • Uncertain

    "Within seconds" is not sourced

    No page we opened gives how long the neutron-capture burst lasts. Merger debris is thrown out within milliseconds to seconds in simulations, so the film says "within seconds" and never "under a second". Neutron captures only have to beat radioactive decay, up to about 100 per second.

  • Uncertain

    The meteorite rain is the favoured story, not a fact

    Tungsten in 3.8-billion-year-old rocks fits a late meteorite rain, but one study argues core-formation chemistry alone could explain the gold in the mantle, and another found rocks that look modern. The "Late Heavy Bombardment" is itself disputed, so the film only says a rain of meteorites came after the core formed.

  • Uncertain

    Earth's core gold is inferred

    The 99.999% figure comes from ruthenium isotopes, not from measuring gold, and other outlets say 99% or 99.9%. The film says "nearly all". The core is mostly iron and nickel with a trace of gold, not a ball of gold.

  • Simplified

    Sugar cubes and teaspoons

    NASA's figure is about a trillion kilograms (a billion tonnes) for a sugar cube of neutron-star matter, so the film says sugar cube. The popular "teaspoon" line is looser: a teaspoon is about five cubes, and the density inside a neutron star is uncertain by a factor of a few. The "gram of neutrons per cubic centimetre" is the r-process neutron density, a different thing.

  • Simplified

    Iron, the iron group, and one analogy

    Fusion ends at the iron group (iron-56 and nickel-62), which the film shortens to "iron". "The ash of a star" is our analogy, used once and dropped. The 5 g ring is our assumption, and "thirteen years before the famous crash" rounds 12.6.

  • Simplified

    Two sets of numbers for the 2004 flare

    NASA says the flare put out more energy in 0.1 seconds than the Sun does in 150,000 years; the discovery paper says 0.2 seconds and 250,000 years. Its distance is given as 42,000 or 50,000 light-years. The film uses NASA's energy line and no distance.

  • How we know

    Some sources are secondary

    The ring's age rests on Space.com snippets and the standard age of the Sun, not on a primary page. Textbook physics (neutrons stick, beta decay climbs the elements, iron sinks to the core) was checked as standard but is not tied to a page. Several quotes reached us through a page-reading tool, so check punctuation on the original pages. The gold-mined figure is the World Gold Council's of November 2026 and will drift.
