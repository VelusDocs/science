Why Is Everything So Expensive Now?

The news says inflation is falling, and your bill doesn't seem to hear. The film follows one extra dollar of cost from a nearly shut strait in the Persian Gulf to your shopping basket, and shows why prices climb like a ratchet. This page lists every factual line in the film, with its source, and says plainly what is a calculation, what is contested and what was true only as of October 2026.

0:00

Why your bill keeps climbing

  1. When the news says inflation is falling, your shopping bill doesn't seem to hear. The same bag of groceries costs more than it did a few years ago, and the old prices are unlikely to come back.

    BLS, Consumer Price Index, September 2026, 14 October 2026 (food at home +1.8% over the year, even while it dipped 0.1% in September). "Unlikely to come back" is about the price level as a whole; see the ratchet notes

  2. A dollar from 2020 buys only about 77 cents' worth of stuff today. (In the description and the thumbnail, not the narration.)

    US consumer prices, January 2020 to September 2026: CPI-U went from 257.971 to 336.940, ×1.306, so a 2020 dollar buys 1 / 1.306 = 77 cents' worth; on the wage-earner index (CPI-W, 251.361 to 330.654, ×1.316) it is 76 centscalc·Index levels, not seasonally adjusted, from BLS, 13 February 2020 (January 2020) and BLS, 14 October 2026 (September 2026)

  3. Inflation, it turns out, isn't how expensive things are, but how fast they're getting more expensive.

    Standard definition: inflation is the rate of change of the price level, usually over twelve months

  4. And once prices in general have climbed, they rarely climb back down, like a ratchet, the tool that turns forwards and locks backwards.

    General economics, and a picture: true of the average price level, not of every price (see the next chapter and the notes)

  5. Sometimes a war on the other side of the planet makes it clatter.

    The 2026 war around the Strait of Hormuz, from the next chapter: IEA commentary, 22 June 2026

  6. And once, in Hungary, it spun so fast that prices doubled between breakfast and bedtime.

    Hanke & Krus, "World Hyperinflations", as summarised in Conversable Economist, 2012 (Hungary, July 1946: prices doubled about every 15 hours; other sources say 15.6)

  7. Who keeps turning it? Let's follow an extra dollar of cost from a narrow strip of sea to your shopping basket.

    Story frame: the travelling dollar is a personification, not a claim

0:56

A thermometer made of groceries

  1. Every month, statisticians fill the same imaginary shopping basket, from bread to rent to haircuts, and check the bill.

    BLS, Consumer Price Index, a monthly index of a fixed basket of goods and services. The UK (ONS), the euro area (Eurostat) and Italy (ISTAT) run their own versions

  2. That basket is a thermometer, the consumer price index, and its rise over the past year is the headline inflation figure on the news.

    BLS, September 2026 ("Over the last 12 months, the all items index increased 3.7 percent")

  3. Now put some money in a drawer and let prices climb three percent a year. A decade later, that money buys only about three quarters of what it used to.

    1.03 to the 10th power is 1.344, so a $100 basket costs $134 and the money buys 1 / 1.344 = 74% of what it didcalc

  1. When inflation falls, the basket doesn't get cheaper. It just climbs more slowly, what economists call disinflation, while prices actually falling, deflation, is rare.

    Standard definitions. Deflation is rare in modern rich economies; Japan and the 1930s are the usual examples

  2. Wages, rents and contracts are hard to cut, and most central banks aim for a gentle climb of about two percent a year, rather than none. Those are the catches that stop the ratchet from slipping.

    The Federal Reserve, the ECB and the Bank of England all target 2%; "most" because not every central bank does. Sticky wages and contracts are textbook macroeconomics, and "catches" is the film's picture; see the notes for the other reasons

  3. Although ratchet isn't exactly right. A few prices, like fuel, can slip back fast when a shock fades, but the basket as a whole rarely does.

    Our research notes: gasoline, eggs, used cars, flights and electronics have all fallen back at times; it is the average level that rarely falls. Fuel moves fast both ways (US gasoline rose 9.1% in September 2026 alone)

  4. Even at that gentle pace, prices double in about thirty-five years, roughly once in a working life.

    1.02 to the 35th power is 2.00. At September's US rate of 3.7%, as the description says, prices would double in ln 2 / ln 1.037 = 19 yearscalc

2:20

The long way to your shelf

  1. In normal times, a fifth of all the oil the world uses sails out of the Persian Gulf through a single narrow doorway, the Strait of Hormuz.

    US EIA, Today in Energy, 16 June 2025 ("oil flow through the strait averaged 20 million barrels per day", "about 20% of global petroleum liquids consumption", 2024 data)

  2. A planet's habit, squeezed through a gap in the map, with barely a back door.

    US EIA (about 2.6 million barrels a day of Saudi and UAE pipeline capacity "could be available to bypass" the strait)·2.6 / 20 = 13% of the normal flowcalc

  1. Then, in early twenty twenty-six, war broke out around the strait, and the doorway nearly slammed shut.

    IEA commentary, 22 June 2026, on "the Strait of Hormuz shock"·The National, 17 July 2026 and Al Jazeera, 23 July 2026 (the war began on 28 February 2026; a near-total blockade). "Nearly" matters: see the notes

  2. From March to May, more than eight barrels in ten that should have sailed through didn't make it.

    IEA commentary, 22 June 2026 (flows fell "from around 20 million barrels per day prior to the conflict" to "an average of 2.7 million barrels per day in March, April and May")·2.7 / 20 = 13.5% got through, so 86.5% did notcalc

  3. Somewhere in that mess, our extra dollar is born, with a simple goal: your shopping basket.

    Story frame, not a claim

  4. Tankers sailing into a war zone need war-risk insurance, cover in case the ship is hit. Before the war, insuring a big tanker for a voyage cost a small slice of its value.

    The National, 17 July 2026 (about 0.25% of hull value before the war, so about $250,000 on a $100 million tanker)

  5. By that summer, the same cover could cost anywhere from a dozen to forty times as much.

    Marcus Baker of the broker Marsh, in The National, 17 July 2026 ("Rates can be anywhere between 3 per cent and 10 per cent on hull value")·3% / 0.25% = 12; 10% / 0.25% = 40calc·Al Jazeera, 23 July 2026, gives 7.5 to 10%. Quotes differ by source and by week

  1. Same ship. Same water. The ship's owner isn't a charity. They pass it on.

    Higher shipping costs reach import and consumer prices: IMF Working Paper 22/61, "Shipping Costs and Inflation", 2022. How much of the insurance bill itself is passed on was not measured; the line states the general mechanism

  2. Meanwhile, at a second chokepoint, the Red Sea, threats kept some container ships on the long way round Africa, about two extra weeks at sea.

    Alphaliner, as reported by Ship & Bunker, 21 October 2026 (5.5 of 8.7 million TEU on Far East-Europe services went via the Red Sea between 15 September and 15 October 2026, so the rest still went round; the Suez route saves about two weeks)·Houthi blockade announcement: Al Jazeera, 23 July 2026

  3. And once again, the shipping line passes it on. By now our dollar has company, extra cents for the insurance, the detour and the fuel. A small crowd of costs, all heading for your basket.

    Story frame built on the lines above; we found no reliable figure for transport's share of a shelf price, so the film gives none

  4. In one big study of shipping-cost spikes, import prices took most of the hit within about two months, but shop prices felt the worst of it about a year later.

    Carrière-Swallow, Deb, Furceri, Jiménez & Ostry, IMF Working Paper 22/61, 2022, and the authors' IMF blog post, 28 March 2022 (import prices respond within about two months; the effect on consumer prices peaks after about a year; doubling shipping costs adds about 0.7 points to inflation)

  5. Fuel itself skipped the slow boat, and pump prices jumped almost at once.

    BLS, September 2026 (gasoline +9.1% in one month; airline fares +22.4% over the year, through jet fuel)·ONS, September 2026 (UK motor fuels +29.8% over the year)

  6. Yet in September, American supermarket food actually got a tiny bit cheaper over the month.

    BLS, September 2026 (food at home −0.1% in the month, still +1.8% over the year)

  7. The thermometer had cracked down the middle. America's headline inflation read three point seven percent, but core inflation, which leaves out jumpy food and energy, read only two point six.

    BLS, September 2026, 14 October 2026 (all items +3.7% over 12 months; all items less food and energy +2.6%; energy +20.4%)

  1. In the euro area, energy alone supplied about forty-five percent of all inflation, a huge share of the fever sitting in a single aisle.

    Eurostat, euro area annual inflation, September 2026, 16 October 2026 (3.8%, with energy contributing 1.71 percentage points; 2.3% without energy)·1.71 / 3.8 = 45%calc

4:22

Not every price takes the boat

  1. Picture a seaside town in August, its hotel rooms exactly the same as in March. The crowd isn't, and the price of a room climbs. That's demand-pull inflation, more money chasing the same amount of stuff.

    Standard textbook definition; the seaside town is our picture

  2. When the world reopened in twenty twenty-one, it was August everywhere at once, just as factories and ports were jammed.

    Bernanke & Blanchard, "What Caused the U.S. Pandemic-Era Inflation?", NBER Working Paper 31417 (price shocks driven by "changes in the level and sectoral composition of demand together with constraints on sectoral supply")

  3. Later, tight job markets pushed wages up too, a slower but longer-lasting click.

    Bernanke & Blanchard (most of the surge came from "shocks to prices given wages"; tight labour markets played a growing, more persistent role later)

  4. How much was crowd and how much was jam is still argued over.

    Shapiro, San Francisco Fed, June 2022 (supply factors a little more than half of the excess in headline inflation; core split about evenly)·St. Louis Fed ("two-thirds" of price growth due to demand)

  5. Then there's money itself. In the nineteen seventies, by its central bank's own account, America let money grow too fast, while an oil embargo quadrupled crude.

    Federal Reserve History, "The Great Inflation" ("policies that allowed for an excessive growth in the supply of money"; "an Arab oil embargo that began in October 1973 and lasted about five months", after which "crude oil prices quadrupled"). Monetary and supply-shock explanations of the 1970s are still debated

  6. Push the printing press to its limit and you get hyperinflation, prices rising more than fifty percent in a single month.

    Hanke & Krus, "World Hyperinflations", Cato Institute, 2012, as summarised in Conversable Economist (an episode starts when the rate of inflation exceeds 50% in a month)

  7. The record isn't the famous German episode, which ranks only around fifth for speed.

    Hanke & Krus, via Conversable Economist (Weimar Germany ranks fifth by highest monthly rate; another version of the table puts it fourth, hence "around")

  8. It's Hungary right after the Second World War, with its breakfast-and-bedtime prices. Saving up was not a strategy.

    Hanke & Krus, via Conversable Economist (Hungary, July 1946, prices doubling about every 15 hours; Zimbabwe, November 2008, about every 25 hours)

  1. Are companies just being greedy? Partly, maybe. In the euro area, profits per item sold made up more than half of home-grown price rises in the last months of twenty twenty-two.

    ECB Economic Bulletin 4/2023, box on unit profits (Hahn, June 2023: unit profits "made a visible contribution to domestic price pressures", about 60% of the growth of the GDP deflator over the three latest quarters, against a 42% share of GDP). "More than half" is the film's rounding down; "the last months of twenty twenty-two" rests on an ECB blog we saw only in a search result, see the notes

  2. But in company accounts, margins stayed broadly unchanged, and profits can swell almost by themselves when every cost jumps at once.

    Same ECB box (mark-ups and margins from company accounts "broadly unchanged"; "unit profits" in national accounts are not corporate profits, and can rise mechanically when input costs surge)

  3. Some see sellers hitching a ride on the shock, others see the shock dragging profits along with it. It isn't settled.

    Our summary of a contested debate: the ECB box above and an IMF study (Hansen, Toscani & Zhou, 2023, seen only in a search summary) measure the profit share; neither settles cause and effect

  4. Some prices don't climb at all. The pack shrinks instead. That's shrinkflation, and it's rarer than it feels: in a British count for twenty sixteen, only about one to two percent of food products shrank.

    ONS, "How many of our products are getting smaller?", 21 January 2019 (in 2016, "between 1% and 2.1% of food products" shrank, and 0.3 to 0.7% grew, from 2,529 price quotes)

  5. Sweets were the exception, and a famous Swiss chocolate bar shaped like a mountain range simply grew wider valleys.

    ONS, "The impact of shrinkflation on CPIH", 24 July 2017 (sugar, jam, syrups, chocolate and confectionery: 1.22 points since 2012). The Toblerone change of 2016, about 12% lighter with wider gaps between the peaks, is from our research notes; neither ONS page names it

  1. At the border, tariffs, taxes on imports, add a toll. How much of America's twenty twenty-five tariffs landed on shoppers is still disputed, from a modest share to nearly the whole toll.

    Amiti, Heise & Weinstein, NBER, July 2026 (about 26% passed through to consumer prices)·Federal Reserve Board, FEDS Note, 8 April 2026 (consistent with full pass-through after about seven months)·St. Louis Fed, 18 August 2026 (effective US tariff rate 11% in late 2025, just below 7% by May 2026)

  2. And once again, at least some of it gets passed on.

    Every estimate above is above zero

6:36

The end of the line

  1. At the end of all these journeys, there's nobody left to pass it on to. Except you. Savings in cash lose, while a fixed-rate loan quietly shrinks against everything else.

    General economics: cash and low-interest savings lose purchasing power at the inflation rate, while a fixed loan repayment shrinks in real terms. Who gains overall depends on wages and interest rates

  2. A grandmother's savings, kept in a tin for a working life at that gentle pace, end up buying about half of what they once could. She did nothing wrong.

    At 2% a year: 1 / 1.02 to the 35th power = 0.50 after 35 years (0.45 after 40)calc·The grandmother is invented

  3. So why is everything so expensive now? Because the price level almost never climbs back down. Today's prices carry every click of the last few years: the reopening crowd, the jammed ports, the energy shocks, and now a nearly shut strait.

    The film's synthesis of the sources above: Bernanke & Blanchard on 2021 to 2023, IEA on 2026. "Almost never" is about the average level, not every price

  4. Central banks mostly pull a single lever, the interest rate. Raise it, and loans get pricier while savings pay a little more.

    Bank of England, "How do higher interest rates help to lower inflation?"

  5. People spend less, the seaside crowd thins out, and a year or more later, prices climb more slowly.

    Bank of England ("it takes time to work. Usually, up to two years"). The seaside crowd is the film's picture

  6. But no interest rate can make oil. Against an energy shock, central banks mostly watch whether it hops off the fuel aisle into wages, and into what people expect next.

    Paraphrase of 2026 ECB and Fed communications seen in search summaries; we did not confirm the September and October 2026 rate decisions, so the film names none

  7. Wages, after all, sit on a ratchet too, held by the same catch.

    Wages are rarely cut in money terms (the stickiness above); a picture, not a measurement

  8. The real question was never whether prices climb, but which ratchet clicks faster. This time, for the average American worker, prices won by a hair. Real pay, what a wage can actually buy, fell by about seven tenths of a percent over the year to September.

    BLS, Real Earnings, September 2026, 14 October 2026 (real average hourly earnings "decreased 0.7 percent from September 2025 to September 2026", seasonally adjusted)

  1. A pay cut that nobody made. When the news says inflation is falling, the price ratchet hasn't stopped. It's only clicking more slowly, and what matters is whether your pay clicks faster.

    The film's conclusion. Note that in September 2026 US inflation actually rose, from 3.4% to 3.7%; the line is about any month when it falls·BLS, September 2026

Simplifications and honest caveats

Eight and a half minutes is short, and the news was still moving when we finished. Here is what we simplified, what is contested and what we could not confirm.

  • Dated

    The Hormuz figures are as of mid-2026

    "More than eight barrels in ten" is the IEA's March to May 2026 average (2.7 against about 20 million barrels a day), published on 22 June 2026. The war began on 28 February 2026; a US-Iran memorandum was signed on 17 June and hostilities resumed in July. In late October, trackers (seen only through search summaries) counted a handful of transits a day against about 85 normally, while an IEA page, also seen only as a search summary, put Gulf crude exports in September back above 75% of pre-war through pipelines, the Red Sea and ship-to-ship transfers. So the strait was "nearly shut" to ships, not to all Gulf oil, and daily counts come from trackers, not official statistics. We did not confirm the strait's status after 26 October 2026.

  • Uncertain

    Insurance quotes vary by source and by week

    The 3 to 10% range is one broker's quote (Marsh, via The National, 17 July 2026); the 0.25% before the war is the article's own figure. Al Jazeera reported 7.5 to 10%; from 1 to 3%; a later broker estimate, seen only in a search summary, gave 6 to 10% against 0.15 to 0.25%. "A dozen to forty times" is our division of the first set; different pairs give different ratios.

  • Simplified

    The ratchet is about the average, not every price

    Fuel, eggs, used cars, flights and electronics can and do get cheaper. What rarely falls is the price level as a whole, for several reasons: wages and contracts are hard to cut, central banks aim for about 2% rather than 0%, and falling prices are feared because they can feed on themselves. The film admits this once ("ratchet isn't exactly right") and says "prices in general" and "the price level" elsewhere.

  • Contested

    Crowd or jam: what caused 2021 to 2023

    Bernanke and Blanchard find that price shocks led and tight labour markets mattered more later. The San Francisco Fed (June 2022) put supply factors at a little more than half of the excess in headline inflation; a St. Louis Fed analysis (2025) put demand at about two-thirds. The film says only that the split "is still argued over".

  • Contested

    Greedflation is not settled

    The ECB box measures "unit profits" in the national accounts, which include self-employed and housing income and depreciation, not company profits. It found them behind roughly 60% of domestic price growth over the three quarters to early 2023, while company-account margins were "broadly unchanged". The film's "last months of twenty twenty-two" comes from an ECB blog we saw only as a search result; the box itself covers three quarters that straddle the turn of the year. Whether firms used the shock or the shock lifted profits mechanically is the open question, which is why the film says "Partly, maybe" and "It isn't settled."

  • Contested

    Tariff pass-through ranges from a quarter to all of it

    The Fed note (April 2026) found pass-through consistent with dollar-for-dollar after about seven months; the NBER paper (July 2026) found about 26%. Both were read through summaries. The St. Louis Fed (August 2026) notes the effective tariff rate fell after the February 2026 Supreme Court ruling and that energy, not tariffs, drove later excess inflation. The film keeps it neutral.

  • Simplified

    One study of shipping costs

    The two months and one year come from one IMF study across many economies, using the Baltic Dry Index, which tracks bulk cargo rather than containers. Our research notes quote "90% within two months" from a VoxEU column we could not open, so the film says only "most of the hit". We found no reliable figure for how much of a shelf price is transport, so the film never gives one.

  • Simplified

    Hyperinflation numbers differ by source

    Hungary 1946 doubles every 15 or 15.6 hours depending on the source; Zimbabwe 2008 every 24.7 or 25 hours. Germany 1923 is fifth in one version of the Hanke and Krus table and fourth in another, so the film says "around fifth". The Cato page with the full table blocked our reader, so the figures come from Timothy Taylor's summary of the paper and the paper is not linked.

  • Simplified

    Shrinkflation is one British count, for one year

    The ONS counted pack-size changes from 2012 to mid-2017; the 1 to 2.1% is for food products in 2016, and only sweets and chocolate showed a clear effect on inflation. Statistical offices adjust prices for pack size, so shrinkflation is mostly counted as inflation already; we did not open the current method pages, so the film does not say so.

  • Simplified

    77 cents is our calculation

    The description and thumbnail say a 2020 dollar buys about 77 cents' worth. We divided US consumer prices in September 2026 by January 2020: CPI-U, the headline index, rose about 1.306 times (77 cents); CPI-W, the wage-earner index, rose 1.316 times (76 cents). It is a US average, unadjusted for season, and the October and November 2025 values are missing because of the 2025 government shutdown, which does not affect this comparison of two months.

  • Left out

    Rent, central-bank decisions and the 1970s comparison

    The description mentions rents that lag a year behind; that line was cut from the film for length. Research by the Cleveland Fed and BLS (seen through search summaries) finds the CPI rent index trails new-tenant rents by about four quarters, partly because most leases run a year. We also left out specific 2026 interest-rate decisions (we could not confirm them), oil prices day by day, and any claim that 2026 is "worse than the 1970s": the IEA's "largest supply disruption" is about volume, not prices adjusted for inflation.

  • Simplified

    Source access

    The research notes named sources without addresses, so on 27 October 2026 we found each page and opened it. Two would not open for us: the VoxEU column on shipping costs and the Cato paper with the full hyperinflation table both returned errors, so neither is linked, and the film's numbers from them are checked against the IMF blog and Timothy Taylor's summary. Some smaller points were seen only in search summaries and are marked as such above: the late-October Hormuz transit counts and rerouted exports, a later insurance estimate, the ECB blog on late 2022, the IMF profits study, 2026 central-bank decisions and the rent-lag research. The ships-round-Africa figures come from Alphaliner through a trade-press report; Alphaliner's own note is not public.
