If They Are the Influencers, You Are the Influenced

Everyone talks about influencers; this film is about the other side of the screen. It runs from Solomon Asch's 1951 line test to recommendation feeds and the teen-wellbeing debate. This page lists every claim the film makes, with its source, and is open about which findings are solid, which are small, and which are still fought over.

The other side of the screen

No factual claims here: the creator holding the face cream is a made-up character, and the chapter ends on a promise ("Let's start in 1951…") that the next chapter keeps.

0:28

Why we copy

  1. In Solomon Asch's 1951 line-matching task, people alone got it wrong less than once in a hundred tries.

    Asch conformity line experiment, Simply Psychology (secondary study guide; control errors 0.7%)

  2. Seated after a row of actors who all named the same wrong line, about a third of the volunteers' answers went along with them.

    Simply Psychology (32%; Asch's 1951 paper gives 36.8%, so "about a third")

  3. Across 133 studies in 17 countries, the crowd's pull rose and fell with culture and era.

    Culture and conformity: a meta-analysis of studies using Asch's line judgement task, Bond & Smith, Psychological Bulletin, 1996

  4. One theory from anthropology: copying whoever everyone watches is a shortcut to finding who knows something useful (prestige bias).

    The evolution of prestige, Henrich & Gil-White, Evolution and Human Behavior, 2001

  5. When researchers gave random posts on a news site one extra upvote, the next reader became 32% likelier to upvote too.

    Social influence bias: a randomized experiment, Muchnik, Aral & Taylor, Science, 2013

1:24

A friend who can't know us back

  1. Researchers named this one-sided closeness "parasocial" in 1956, after viewers who felt they knew the TV and radio hosts talking to them.

    Horton & Wohl, Psychiatry, 1956 (the essay that coined "parasocial interaction")·Parasocial relationships can tell us a lot about the social brain, Scientific American, 2024

  2. Brain scans hint that for people who get lost in stories, TV characters stir the brain's social region almost like real acquaintances.

    Scientific American, 2024 (Dylan Wagner's lab, Ohio State; no effect sizes given)

  3. That may be why a familiar tip can feel more trustworthy than an ad, though nobody has measured it cleanly.

    No clean study found: a reasoned mechanism (familiarity, prestige, a post that looks unpaid), told as a "may"

  4. By one trade body's estimate, spending on influencer marketing grew roughly 19-fold in under a decade, to about 33 billion dollars in 2025.

    Influencer Marketing Benchmark Report 2025, Influencer Marketing Hub, 2025 ($32.55bn; $1.7bn in 2016 via secondary lists)

  5. In 2017, among influencers the US regulator had already warned, only about one in five sponsored posts clearly said it was an ad.

    Public Citizen report on FTC-warned influencers, 2017 (84 of 412 posts; via secondary coverage)

  6. In 2019, a thousand fake followers could cost just 16 dollars.

    Fake followers in influencer marketing will cost $1.3 billion in 2019, CNBC, 2019 (CHEQ / University of Baltimore estimate)

2:27

The strings

  1. An algorithm now helps pick the faces we see: in one volunteer study, about seven in ten of the YouTube videos people reported regretting had been recommended to them.

    YouTube Regrets: findings, Mozilla Foundation, 2021 (71% of 3,362 reports from 1,662 volunteers)

  2. Each extra moral-emotional word, like "hate", made a political tweet spread about 20% further.

    Brady et al., PNAS, 2017 (PMC5514704)

  3. People rewarded with likes for outrage went on to post more of it.

    Brady et al., Science Advances, 2021 (12.7 million tweets; via search extract)

  4. So the audience trains the poster and the poster trains the audience: it seems the influencers are the influenced too.

    Brady et al., 2017 and 2021 (studied ordinary users, so applying it to creators is our extrapolation; hence "it seems")

  5. In a 2019 poll, American and British kids were about three times as likely to want to be YouTubers as astronauts.

    More children dream of being YouTubers than astronauts, Lego says, CNBC, 2019 (Lego / Harris Poll)

3:10

What it costs us, and the choice we still have

  1. If the prestige theory is right, some desires arrive borrowed, copied from faces that are watched rather than wise.

    The evolution of prestige, Henrich & Gil-White, 2001 (a theory; whether prestige tracks skill is its weak link)

  2. Heavy photo-sharing use is linked to worse body image, though most studies can't tell which comes first.

    Holland & Tiggemann, Body Image, 2016: a systematic review of 20 studies (PubMed 26995158)

  3. In one small brain-scan study, photos with many made-up likes lit up teenagers' reward circuitry more, and the teens liked them more too.

    Neuroscience News on Sherman et al., Psychological Science, 2016

  4. Whether social media harms teenagers is fiercely debated.

    Odgers, review of Haidt's "The Anxious Generation", Nature, 2024, and Haidt's reply (via secondary coverage)

  5. One analysis of over 355,000 adolescents tied technology use to just 0.4% of the differences in wellbeing, about as much as eating potatoes.

    Technology use explains at most 0.4% of adolescent wellbeing, Oxford Internet Institute, 2019 (Orben & Przybylski, Nature Human Behaviour)

  6. But an average can hide the few who are hurt badly.

    Oxford Internet Institute, 2019 (our research notes' caveat on averages and subgroups)

  7. The US Surgeon General warned that more than three hours a day was linked to twice the risk of depression and anxiety symptoms in young teens.

    US Surgeon General's advisory on social media and youth mental health, May 2023 (12 to 15-year-olds; via NPR, ABC and others)

  8. The average teen spends about three and a half hours a day on social media, some eighty waking days a year.

    US Surgeon General's advisory, 2023·our arithmetic: 3.5 h × 365 ÷ 16 waking hours = 80 dayscalc

  9. Experiments where people cut back for a few weeks found small gains in wellbeing, but they were short.

    Hunt et al., University of Pennsylvania, 2018 (143 students, 3 weeks)·Allcott et al., American Economic Review, 2020 (2,743 adults, about 4 weeks)

  10. So harm to a whole generation is still unproven, which isn't the same as disproven.

    Odgers (Nature, 2024) and Haidt: correlation dominates, and both sides back some platform changes

  11. In one study of stock-picking influencers, more than half gave advice that did worse than the market, yet they had more followers than the skilled ones.

    Finfluencers, Kakhbod et al., Swiss Finance Institute Research Paper 23-30 (abstract read; a working paper on one platform)

  12. Copying is a huge part of how humans learn, one borrowed idea at a time.

    Henrich & Gil-White, 2001

  13. In Asch's room, the crowd's pull dropped sharply as soon as one other person named the right line.

    Simply Psychology (conformity fell to 5.5% with one ally; Asch 1951/1956)

Simplifications and honest caveats

This is a field of small effects, self-reports and heated debate. Here is what we simplified, what is uncertain, and what is contested.

  • Simplified

    Asch did not prove we are sheep

    Conformity varied with culture and era, and collapsed when one person dissented. Many volunteers knew the answer and went along in public. Our Asch numbers come through a study guide; the original papers were not read.

  • Uncertain

    Brain scans of fictional characters, not creators

    The parasocial brain work used TV characters, with no effect sizes in the article we read. "The brain can't tell an influencer from a friend" is false as stated, so the film only says the scans "hint".

  • Uncertain

    Industry and vendor numbers

    The $33 billion market figure is a trade body's estimate; its 2014 baseline could not be confirmed, so we used 2016. The $16 fake-follower price is a 2019 vendor estimate.

  • Simplified

    Samples that are not the whole world

    The "one in five" disclosure figure covers influencers already warned by the FTC. Mozilla's regret reports came from self-selected volunteers. The upvote study is one news site. The Twitter studies are about political tweets.

  • Simplified

    "The influencers are the influenced"

    The outrage-feedback studies measured ordinary users, not creators. Applying the loop to creators is our extrapolation, which is why the line says "it seems".

  • Contested

    Does social media harm teenagers?

    Fiercely debated. The 0.4% figure is an average built on self-reported screen time, and it can hide a vulnerable few. The Surgeon General's "twice the risk" is a correlation. Cut-back experiments are short. Harm is unproven, not disproven.

  • Uncertain

    Small and single studies

    The likes-and-reward study had a small sample (we did not confirm its size, so none is given) and it shows a reward response, not addiction. The finfluencer result is a working paper on one platform.

  • Left out

    Claims we chose not to make

    "Likes are a drug", "fake news is spread by bots", and "Facebook knew Instagram destroys girls" are all overstated or mixed in the evidence, so the film avoids them. We found no solid figures on influencer health misinformation, so it says nothing about it.
