How Do Rotor Sails Work?

Some of the biggest ships on Earth are growing giant spinning towers: sails with no cloth. The film explains how a spinning cylinder bends the wind into a push, what is inside the tower, why the idea sailed to New York in 1926 and was then benched for most of a century, and why it is coming back as fuel and carbon get dearer. This page lists every factual line in the film, with its source, and says plainly which fuel savings were checked by a third party, which are the maker's own figures, and what is our own arithmetic.

What the film claims, and where it comes from

In film order, one or two sentences per line, in the words of the narration. calc means we derived the number ourselves and re-ran the arithmetic. The football, the bench, the assists and the axolotl are metaphors and decoration; the tanker is drawn, not filmed. Several sources (gCaptain, Lloyd's Register's 2025 release, Maritime Executive, IMarEST, the Hudson River Maritime Museum, Wikipedia's Magnus effect page) are named in our research notes without a web address, so they are listed here by name, without a link. Some figures were seen only in search summaries; the notes say which. Chapter times follow the final cut.

0:00

Sails with no cloth

  1. This is an oil tanker, and the two towers on its deck are spinning, each as tall as a ten-storey building.

    Norsepower, Rotor Sails confirmed savings of 8.2% fuel in Maersk Pelican project, 24 October 2019 (Maersk Tankers' 109,647 dwt LR2 product tanker, "Two 30-metre tall Rotor Sails")·30 m ÷ about 3 m a storey = 10 storeyscalc

  2. Why are some of the biggest ships on Earth growing giant spinning towers?

    Marine Log, Anemoi completes world's largest yet rotor sail retrofit (five 35 m × 5 m folding rotors on Vale's 400,000 dwt ore carrier Sohar Max, October 2024; seen in a search summary)·gCaptain, 15 September 2026 ("five 35-meter rotor sails, already fitted"), from our research notes

  3. They're rotor sails, sails with no cloth. They spin, and the spin turns the wind into a push that saves the ship some fuel.

    Norsepower, Rotor Sail product page (a cylinder on deck turned by an electric motor; the rotation makes the air faster on one side than the other). The saving is claim 34. "Some" fuel, because the motor uses power too

  4. And fuel is often a third or more of what it costs to run a ship.

    World Shipping Council, 2008 (fuel as much as 50 to 60% of operating cost, depending on ship type); a 2022 TI Insight brief (30 to 50% at $100 a barrel); both from our research notes. The shares use different denominators, hence "often a third or more"

  5. Part of that bill hides in the price of your sneakers, which probably crossed an ocean on a cargo ship.

    Maritime Executive, 17 March 2026 ("The extra costs will eventually get passed on to charterers, cargo owners and consumers"), from our research notes. The sneakers are our example

  6. Spinning towers sound new, but ships first sailed on them a century ago, then gave them up.

    DLR Magazine 177, A voyage of rotating cylinders (German Aerospace Center: the first rotor-ship voyages in October 1924, Prandtl and Betz aboard in Kiel in November 1924). Why they were given up is claims 28 and 29

0:46

A football the size of a building

  1. Isaac Newton noticed that a spun tennis ball swerves, just like a footballer's banana kick curls around the wall.

    Wikipedia, Magnus effect (Newton in 1672, watching tennis players at a Cambridge college; Roberto Carlos's banana kick as a Magnus-curved football)·IMarEST, "Rotor sails put a new spin on Flettner", 9 July 2021 (also gives Newton, 1672)

  2. Almost two centuries later, a physicist named Magnus made a spinning brass cylinder swerve in a blast of air, the Magnus effect.

    DLR Magazine 177 ("In 1852, Heinrich Gustav Magnus showed that a rotating cylinder in an airstream experiences a 'transverse force.' Magnus could not fully explain it")·Wikipedia, Magnus effect ("a rapidly rotating brass cylinder and an air blower")·1852 − 1672 = 180 yearscalc

  3. A rotor sail is that curling football, stretched into a tower.

    Wikipedia, Flettner rotor ("a right circular cylinder with disc end plates which is spun along its long axis"). The football is a metaphor

  4. As the tower spins, it drags a thin skin of air around with it. On the side spinning with the wind, the skin speeds the air up, and on the other side, it slows the air down.

    NASA Glenn, Lift of a rotating cylinder ("the molecules of the air will stick to the surface"; the flow over one side "is assisted by the induced flow", the other "is opposed")

  5. So the wind gets bent one way, and the tower gets shoved the other. Newton again: action and reaction.

    NASA Glenn ("The net turning of the flow has produced an upward force"). NASA's model is a simplified one without viscosity; see the notes

  6. But the wind doesn't spin the rotor. A motor does. Stop the motor, and the shove almost disappears.

    Norsepower, Rotor Sail (a cylinder turned by an electric motor, not by the wind)·Wikipedia, Rotor ship (the rotor is driven by its own power source, not the wind). A cylinder that is not spinning makes almost no Magnus force

  7. With the motor on, it pushes much harder than a cloth sail of the same size.

    Hudson River Maritime Museum (Steven Woods, 23 December 2022: "much more thrust from the same surface area"), from our research notes·Norsepower ("about ten times more efficient than a conventional sail", a company claim with no basis given). The film deliberately gives no number

  8. It works best with the wind from the side, and head-on, it barely helps.

    Wikipedia, Flettner rotor (the force is "at right angles to the airflow and the rotation axis", so a side wind pushes the ship ahead and a head wind pushes it sideways). A CFD study, seen in a search summary, found the most thrust with the apparent wind about 120° off the bow

1:51

Inside the spinning tower

  1. In Norsepower's design, the rotor is a hollow drum of glass and carbon fibre, built around a foam core of recycled plastic.

    CompositesWorld, Modernizing the mechanical rotor sail, about December 2020 (resin-infused sandwich tubes of carbon and stitched glass fabric around a Gurit Kerdyn Green core, "100% recycled PET")

  2. An electric motor turns it, and on top sits a wide disc, the end plate, that helps stop air spilling over the rim from one side to the other.

    CompositesWorld (an internal steel shaft rotates the sail, driven by an electric motor)·DLR Magazine 177 (Albert Betz "added end plates to the cylinder ends")·Why: high-pressure air bleeds round the end to the low-pressure side; Seifert's 2012 review, seen in a search summary. How much end plates help "remains unclear"

  3. Some towers tilt down on hydraulic hinges for low bridges and cargo cranes.

    Norsepower, Rotor Sail (the Tilting Foundation, sold from June 2020, "hydraulic hinges" lower the sail to horizontal for bridges or loading)

  4. A control system reads the wind, picks a speed, and flips the spin when the wind swaps sides.

    Norsepower, EUR 2.6m funding release, 2016 (the system senses when the wind is strong enough to save fuel and starts the rotors itself)·Wikipedia, Rotor ship ("Reversing tack requires reversing rotor rotation")

  5. But the wind it reads isn't the true wind. A moving ship makes its own breeze, and the rotor feels the two added together, the apparent wind.

    Standard vector addition·a ship making 15 knots in a 15-knot side wind feels the wind about 45° off the bowcalc

  6. It makes lift at right angles to that wind, plus some drag along it, and whatever points forward is thrust.

    Wikipedia, Flettner rotor ("at right angles to the airflow and the rotation axis")·Forward thrust = lift × sin(angle) − drag × cos(angle), with the angle of the apparent wind measured from the bow: textbook geometry

  7. Spin the skin faster than the wind, usually two to four times faster, and the lift grows, up to a point.

    Beijing Institute of Technology, An assisted propulsion device of vessel utilizing wind energy (thrust peaked at a spin ratio of 2.5) and two other studies (a Korean study rising from spin ratio 2 to 4; a CFD study with peak thrust at 3.0), all seen in search summaries. No single source gives one operating ratio, hence "usually"

  8. That spin takes power, and each motor draws up to about a hundred and sixty kilowatts.

    IMO Future Fuels, rotor sails (drive power "from 40 kW up to 160 kW" depending on size and top speed; a generic range seen in a search excerpt, the page returned an error when opened)

  9. But at their best, their maker says, the big rotors on one cargo ship pushed like seven megawatts of engine.

    Norsepower, Sea-Cargo's tilting Rotor Sails deliver instant operational results, April 2021 (two 35 m sails on the ro-ro SC Connector; "maximum thrust force equivalent to 7 MW of propeller shaft power" in testing). A peak, estimated and company-reported

  1. Hundreds of kilowatts in, megawatts out, at the peak.

    2 rotors × up to 160 kW = up to 320 kW, against the 7 MW peakcalc·The comparison joins two sources: the IMO's generic motor range and SC Connector's company-reported peak. SC Connector's own motor power is not published

3:10

Benched for a century

  1. Back in the nineteen twenties, a German engineer, Anton Flettner, swapped a schooner's sails for spinning towers.

    DLR Magazine 177 (Flettner commissioned the Germania yard in Kiel; trials in 1924)·Hudson River Maritime Museum (the Buckau was "originally built as an auxiliary sailing schooner in 1920"); IMarEST (German patent filed September 1922)

  2. A research lab did the aerodynamics, but Flettner patented the idea without telling them, and got the headlines.

    DLR Magazine 177 (Flettner "had filed a patent for the application without informing the AVA", the Göttingen institute of Prandtl and Betz; the press credited Flettner alone)

  3. In nineteen twenty-six, a century ago, his rotor ship crossed the Atlantic to New York.

    Wikipedia, Rotor ship (left 31 March 1926 via South America, arrived in New York on 9 May)·IMarEST (renamed Baden-Baden, "sailed for New York in 1926")

  1. So why didn't the world copy it? By most accounts, money. Freight rates collapsed, the great crash hit, and cheap oil kept diesel engines hard to beat.

    Hudson River Maritime Museum ("collapse of shipping rates in the 1920s did not provide an incentive for building more ships"); IMarEST (it "could not compete economically with diesel engines")·Wikipedia (the 1929 crash and cheap marine diesel)

  2. A rotor took too long to pay for itself, so the idea was benched.

    Wikipedia (payback "too long"). No source gives a payback figure for the 1920s, hence "by most accounts"

  3. The ship got her cloth sails back, and in nineteen thirty-one, she sank in a Caribbean storm.

    DLR Magazine 177 (converted back to a three-masted schooner after about two years; sank in 1931 "in a storm in the Caribbean")·IMarEST (a Caribbean storm in 1931)

  4. The rotors worked. The money didn't.

    Our research summary: no source records an accident or technical failure; the three sources above blame money. Some sources say the spin power was out of proportion to the thrust, see the notes

  5. So the real question isn't whether a spinning tower works, but what a tonne of fuel costs.

    The film's reading of claims 28 and 29

  6. In twenty eighteen, the rotor got back on the pitch, on a year-long trial aboard our tanker, the Maersk Pelican.

    Norsepower, 24 October 2019 (installed in August 2018; measured from 1 September 2018 to 1 September 2019). Interest had also returned after the 1970s oil crisis, see the notes

  7. The rotors' maker reported that they saved eight point two percent of the ship's fuel, with the data validated by Lloyd's Register.

    Norsepower ("the aggregated total fuel saved from 1 September 2018 to 1 September 2019 was 8.2% savings. This is equivalent to approximately 1,400 tonnes of CO2"; Lloyd's Register's Ship Performance Group "analysed and validated the performance data")·Hellenic Shipping News (the same release). Company-reported; the baseline was modelled

  8. That's roughly one tonne in every twelve left in the tank, and each tonne is now worth more.

    1 ÷ 0.082 = 12.2calc·1,400 t of CO2 ÷ 3.114 t of CO2 per tonne of heavy fuel oil ≈ 450 t of fuel saved in the yearcalc·Why each tonne is worth more: the next two claims

  9. Since twenty twenty-four, Europe has charged big ships for a growing share of their carbon.

    European Commission, FAQ on maritime transport in the EU Emissions Trading System (ships of 5,000 GT and above; 40% of emissions for 2024, 70% for 2025, 100% from 2026; voyages to or from non-EU ports count half)

  10. And this spring, with the Strait of Hormuz in crisis, ship fuel in Singapore roughly doubled, to around a thousand dollars a tonne.

    Maritime Executive, 17 March 2026 (Singapore bunkers "above $1,100 per tonne"); other reports seen in search summaries give a peak of $1,053 a tonne on 20 March against about $540 before; from our research notes·1,053 ÷ 540 = 1.95calc

Just an assist

  1. Today, a little over a hundred big merchant ships carry wind power of any kind.

    gCaptain, 15 September 2026, citing the International Windship Association ("more than 100 large merchant ships are now equipped with modern wind propulsion systems", more than 5 million dwt); Ship & Bunker, 12 June 2025 (70 in operation a year earlier, about 130 on order); from our research notes

  2. That's about one ship in a thousand.

    100 ÷ 112,500 ships = 0.09%; by deadweight, 5 million ÷ 2,440 million dwt = 0.2%calc·World fleet from UNCTAD's Review of Maritime Transport 2025, as reported secondhand in our notes

  3. Bridges, cranes and ports limit how tall a tower can stand, and the savings swing with the route.

    gCaptain, 2026 ("Bridges, cranes and terminals can restrict how tall or where equipment can be installed"; some routes suit wind better), from our research notes·The swing is claim 41

  4. On one windy crossing of the North Pacific, a bulk carrier's rotors saved twenty-one percent. Its long-term average was about nine.

    Lloyd's Register press release with Anemoi, 20 May 2025 (TR Lady, an 82,000 dwt bulk carrier with three 24 m rotors: 9.1% verified long-term average net fuel saving; 21% on one 22-day North Pacific voyage, which the release says should not be the headline), from our research notes

  1. Rotors share the pitch with suction wings, rigid sails and kites, and every one of these ships still burns fuel.

    gCaptain, 2026 (four 26 m suction sails on the juice carrier Atlantic Orchard; rigid wing sails); SkySails towing kites (a 160 m² kite in 2008); Ship & Bunker, 2025 (rotor sails the most used type); all from our research notes·TR Lady still gets about 91% of its propulsion from fuel

  2. Because as fuel and carbon get dearer, a century-old trick is starting to pay.

    The film's answer, drawn from claims 34, 36 and 37. It is an inference: no source gives a reliable payback figure, and no source ties the 2026 fuel spike to rotor orders. Hence "starting to"

  3. Spin a tower, bend the wind, and fuel stays in the tank. The rotor doesn't score the goals, it makes the assists.

    A summary of claims 10, 11 and 35, and a metaphor: a rotor assists the engine, it does not replace it

  4. The rotors still work, and this time, the money is starting to work too. Next time you see a tower spinning on a ship, you're looking at the price of fuel.

    The channel's view, not a claim

  5. For most of a century, oil was simply cheap enough to leave the rotor on the bench.

    Claims 28 and 29; "most of a century" is loose, since research returned after the 1970s oil crisis and Enercon's E-Ship 1 sailed with four rotors from 2010 (Hudson River Maritime Museum, Wikipedia, from our notes)

Simplifications and honest caveats

Six minutes is short. Here is what we left out, simplified, calculated ourselves or could not confirm.

  • Company-reported

    The 8.2% is the maker's figure, with checked data

    The Maersk Pelican result was announced by Norsepower, which sold the rotors. Lloyd's Register analysed and validated the performance data, but we found no report published by Lloyd's Register itself. The saving is measured against a baseline built from measurements before fitting plus computer modelling, because a ship's fuel use also swings with weather, hull fouling, speed and trim. The forecast had been 7 to 10%, so the result sat inside it. The film says "the rotors' maker reported" and names Lloyd's Register.

  • Verified, with limits

    TR Lady: a verified method, and one lucky voyage

    The 9.1% long-term average was verified by Lloyd's Register, which reviewed Anemoi's method and model over eight laden and ballast legs and more than 167 on/off tests; it is not a fleet-wide measurement, and the release was issued with the maker. The 21% came from a single 22-day North Pacific voyage, and the release itself says a favourable route should not be the headline. The film gives both numbers.

  • Company-reported

    Seven megawatts is a peak, not an average

    SC Connector's "7 MW of propeller shaft power" is Norsepower's estimate of a peak in testing, three months after fitting, described as initial findings still being monitored. No third party checked it. Higher averages sometimes quoted for this ship (20 to 25%, up to 70% on some voyages) are the operator's or a pre-installation estimate, and the film does not use them.

  • Uncertain

    The honest range of savings

    Checked rotor results sit around 8 to 9% (Pelican, TR Lady); the first verified result, on the ro-ro Estraden in 2015, was 2.6%. The biggest installation, the 400,000 dwt Sohar Max with five 35 m rotors, has only a projection ("as much as 6%"). Company figures run higher. Savings depend on the route, the wind and the ship.

  • Simplified

    "Hundreds of kilowatts in, megawatts out"

    The 160 kW is a generic range from an IMO explainer (40 to 160 kW depending on size), seen in a search excerpt because the page itself returned an error. It is not SC Connector's motor, whose power is not published. The line joins two sources and is meant as an order of magnitude, at the peak.

  • Left out

    "Ten times the lift of a sail"

    Rotor makers and a vendor encyclopedia say a rotor gives about ten times the lift of a cloth sail of the same area. We found no measurement behind it; the real figure depends on spin ratio, end plates and scale. The film only says "much harder".

  • Simplified

    The physics, in one breath

    "Drags a skin of air" is the boundary layer; real flow around a spinning cylinder separates and becomes unsteady, and NASA's teaching model leaves out viscosity while still giving a good first estimate. The film avoids the popular "air has to go faster to meet on the other side" story, which is wrong. The spin ratio of two to four is from several studies seen only in search summaries; how much end plates help is still debated in the literature.

  • Simplified

    Inside the tower: one design, not all

    The materials are Norsepower's (an internal shaft turns the sail). The exploded view on screen shows a generic layout from a patent (EP3409573), in which the drum spins around a fixed inner tower on bearings; other makers differ. Rotor speeds, bearing types and foundation loads for current rotors are not published, so the film gives none.

  • Contested

    Flettner's ship: details the sources disagree on

    The Buckau's rotors were about 15 m tall and 3 m wide in IMarEST and Wikipedia, 18 m and 2.8 m in DLR. Its spin motors were 37 kW each in one account and 7.5 kW in another, and some sources say the spin power was out of proportion to the thrust. A widely repeated claim that the 1926 crossing used 12 tonnes of fuel against 45 for a motor ship rests on one weak source, so the film leaves it out. The exact 1926 dates come from Wikipedia only.

  • Simplified

    "Benched for a century" is shorthand

    The idea came back after the 1970s oil crisis, in research papers and a few trial ships, and Enercon's E-Ship 1 sailed with four rotors from 2010. The Maersk Pelican trial was the first big, independently checked result on a tanker, not the first rotor since 1926.

  • Uncertain

    Fuel prices and the Hormuz spike

    Prices differ by hub and by fuel grade: reports give Singapore peaks of $1,053 to over $1,100 a tonne in March 2026 against about $540 before, and one series averaged around $960. Hence "roughly doubled, to around a thousand dollars". We found no source showing that the spike caused rotor orders, and the film does not say so.

  • Uncertain

    "One ship in a thousand" mixes two counts

    The Windship Association counts large merchant ships; UNCTAD's 112,500 includes smaller ones, and the dates differ by 20 months. By count it is about 0.09%, by deadweight about 0.2%. Not all of the hundred-plus ships carry rotors: suction wings, rigid sails and kites are included.

  • Contested

    Does it pay yet?

    We found no reliable price or payback for a rotor sail: only sellers' projections from 2011 and a consultant's warning that payback can run "well beyond a typical charter contract", because the shipowner pays for the rotor while the charterer saves the fuel. Europe's carbon charge (40% of emissions in 2024, 70% in 2025, all from 2026, half for voyages to or from outside the EU) adds to the incentive, and the IMO's planned global carbon price was postponed in October 2025; we could not find the result of the October 2026 vote. "Is starting to pay" is as far as the evidence goes.

  • Simplified

    Fuel's share of the bill

    Estimates of fuel's share of running costs range from 30 to 50% to as much as 85% of voyage costs on some tankers, measured against different totals. "Often a third or more" is the safe floor. The sneakers are an illustration, not a calculation.

  • Simplified

    Source access

    We read the Norsepower releases and product page, the DLR magazine, NASA Glenn, CompositesWorld, the European Commission FAQ and the Wikipedia pages directly. The IMO motor range, the spin-ratio studies, the end-plate review and several fleet and price figures were seen only in search summaries. gCaptain, Lloyd's Register's 2025 release, Maritime Executive, Ship & Bunker, IMarEST, the Hudson River Maritime Museum and the World Shipping Council are named in our research with quotes but without web addresses, so they are not linked here.

  • Left out

    Stylisation, and what we left out

    The football, the bench, the scoreboard and the axolotl are decoration; the tanker and the 1926 ship are drawn, not to scale. Left out: Barbara, the 1926 three-rotor freighter; the Viking Grace ferry's detailed results; Vale's Sohar Max projection; shipping's share of world greenhouse gases (2.89% in 2018, IMO); the IMO vote; and any payback figure.
