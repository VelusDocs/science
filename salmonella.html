Salmonella's Perfect Inside Job

What the Short claims, and where it comes from

In film order, one line each. "Burglar", "lock pick" and "inside job" are metaphors; the claims under them are not. Sources named without a link are ones our research notes cite without a page address; search them by title.

0:00

The burglar in your fridge

  1. More than one in twenty-five packs of chicken at the grocery store carries Salmonella.

    Salmonella: about, CDC, 2024 ("more than 1 in every 25 packages"; a contamination rate on raw meat, not a rate of illness)

  2. Salmonella is America's deadliest food germ.

    Estimates of foodborne illness in the United States, CDC (the leading cause of US foodborne deaths, about 238 a year, among about 1.28 million illnesses)

0:11

The lock pick and the stormy sea

  1. Salmonella carries a hollow needle that it pushes into a gut cell (the type III secretion system).

    Cryo-EM structure of the needle complex, Hu et al., Nature Communications, 2018 ("a continuous channel")·Lou et al., Frontiers in Cellular and Infection Microbiology, 2019

  2. Proteins pumped through the needle make the cell's surface ruffle up and swallow the bacterium whole.

    Wang & Zhou, Journal of Microbes and Infection, 2009 (SopE, SopB, SipA and SipC rearrange the cell's actin into "membrane ruffles that engulf the bacterium")

0:24

Someone on the inside

  1. Your immune system's alarm, inflammation, ends up helping the burglar, an inflammation Salmonella itself provokes.

    Gut inflammation provides a respiratory electron acceptor for Salmonella, Winter et al., Nature, 2010

  2. The inflammation turns a sulphur compound already in your gut (thiosulfate) into a brand-new chemical (tetrathionate).

    Winter et al., 2010 (reactive oxygen made during inflammation reacts with thiosulfate in the gut)

0:36

The burglar's air

  1. Salmonella can breathe the new chemical.

    Winter et al., 2010 (tetrathionate is "a new respiratory electron acceptor": anaerobic respiration, not eating)

  2. Your friendly fermenting gut bacteria can't, so Salmonella out-competes them.

    Winter et al., 2010 (the fermenting gut microbes are out-competed, not poisoned)

  3. In mice, that trick alone meant about eighty times more Salmonella.

    Winter et al., 2010 ("approximately 80-fold" more than a mutant that can't use tetrathionate, 4 days after infection, in a mouse colitis model)

  4. For most healthy people, the symptoms are over within about a week.

    Salmonella: signs and symptoms, CDC (symptoms usually last 4 to 7 days; see a doctor for bloody diarrhoea, high fever, dehydration, or diarrhoea or vomiting that lasts more than 2 days)

0:49

Your kitchen, your choice

  1. Cook chicken through, and check it with a thermometer.

    USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service and the CDC's chicken safety advice: poultry is safe at an internal 165 °F (74 °C)

  2. Rinsing raw chicken can splash bacteria around your sink.

    USDA observational study, 2019: of people who washed raw poultry, 60% had bacteria in the sink afterwards, and 26% got them onto a ready-to-eat salad (via press coverage of the USDA release)

  3. The inside job needs you: your food and, in a way, your own alarm.

    Rhetoric that sums up the claims above, not a new claim

Simplifications and honest caveats

A minute is short. Here is what we left out or simplified.

  • Uncertain

    The 80× comes from mice

    It was measured in a mouse colitis model (mice pre-treated with an antibiotic), 4 days after infection. Later work supports the same idea in people, but there is no human "80×", so the Short says "in mice" and shows no human number.

  • Simplified

    "Deadliest" means most deaths, not most dangerous per case

    Salmonella causes more US foodborne deaths than any other germ because it is so common. Per case it is rarely deadly: about 1 in 100 people is hospitalised and about 1 in 5,400 dies. One in twenty-five chicken packs is about contamination of raw meat; cooking kills it.

  • Simplified

    The break-in

    The needle delivers proteins, and the cell then swallows the bacterium with its own machinery. Salmonella also has other ways in, and a second needle system for surviving inside the cell. We don't give the needle's size because our research could not confirm one.

  • Simplified

    "Your immune system helps"

    Salmonella provokes the inflammation itself. The immune system isn't on its side; Salmonella exploits a side effect of its alarm.

  • Uncertain

    The sink study

    The USDA rinsing result is observational, and our research reached it through press coverage. The advice not to rinse holds either way, because rinsing doesn't kill the bacteria; only cooking does.

  • Left out

    Typhoid, and the other places Salmonella hides

    Typhoid fever is caused by a different, human-only Salmonella and is not this story. Salmonella also comes from eggs, produce, raw dough, pet turtles and backyard poultry, not only chicken.
