What If You Fell Into a Black Hole?

Fall into a small black hole and you are torn apart long before you reach it. Fall into a giant one, like M87*, and you cross the point of no return without noticing a thing. This page lists every claim the film makes, where each one comes from, and what we had to simplify on the way down.

What the film claims, and where it comes from

In film order, one line each. Links go to the sources our researchers read. calc means we derived the number ourselves from a standard formula and a fact-checker recomputed it. Wikipedia pages are secondary sources; we used them for textbook formulas and checked their numbers.

0:00

The first image of a black hole

  1. In 2019, astronomers released the first image of a black hole's shadow: a dark disc inside a ring of glowing gas. The black hole itself is never seen.

    First image of a black hole, M87* (press release eso1907), ESO / Event Horizon Telescope, 2019

  2. The event horizon hiding inside that shadow is smaller than the shadow (about 2.5 times), but still wide enough to hold the orbits of all our planets.

    eso1907, ESO, 2019 ("just under 40 billion km across")·What are black holes?, NASA, no date ("out to well beyond the planets")·Photon sphere, Wikipedia

  3. Whatever crosses the event horizon, even light, cannot come back out.

    What are black holes?, NASA·Inside black holes: the Schwarzschild black hole, Andrew Hamilton, JILA / University of Colorado, no date

0:29

The Squeeze

  1. Anything becomes a black hole if you squeeze it inside a small enough radius, its Schwarzschild radius.

    Schwarzschild radius, Wikipedia

  2. Squeezed far enough, the Earth would be the size of a marble (about 1.8 cm across).

    Schwarzschild radius, Wikipedia ("approximately 9 mm" radius)·our calculationcalc

  3. The Moon would shrink to a grain of sand (about 0.2 mm across).

    Schwarzschild radius, Wikipedia·our calculation; the sand comparison is ourscalc

  4. The Sun would become a ball about 6 km across, a bit over an hour's brisk walk (5.9 km at 5 km/h).

    Schwarzschild radius, Wikipedia ("approximately 3.0 km" radius)·our arithmetic for the walkcalc

  5. A person would have to be crushed to about a ten-billionth of a proton's width.

    our calculation: 2.1 × 10⁻²⁵ m for 70 kg, against a proton about 1.7 × 10⁻¹⁵ m widecalc·formula: Schwarzschild radius, Wikipedia

  6. In nature, the black holes we know are made when a dying heavy star's core collapses under its own gravity.

    What are black holes?, NASA (stellar-mass black holes, "three to dozens of times the Sun's mass")

  7. No black hole has been confirmed below about 3 Suns. The lightest candidate, GW230529 (about 3.6 Suns), might even be a heavy neutron star.

    GW230529, Wikipedia·GW230529 and the 3 to 5 solar-mass "lower mass gap", Xing et al., ApJ / arXiv, 2024

1:19

Size Matters

  1. A typical stellar black hole of about 10 Suns has an event horizon about 60 km across, half an hour by car at motorway speed.

    What are black holes?, NASA (range of stellar masses)·our calculation: 59 km; 32 minutes at 110 km/hcalc

  2. About 8,000 km out, the hole already pulls an astronaut's feet harder than their head by one Earth gravity (for a 1.8 m body).

    our calculation: 1 g at 7,870 km, tidal formula Δa = 2GML/r³calc·"roughly London to Beijing": editorial comparison (about 8,100 km), not sourced in our research

  3. The body is stretched lengthwise and squeezed in from the sides.

    Inside black holes, Andrew Hamilton, JILA ("the gravity at your feet is stronger than the gravity at your head"; "crushed in the horizontal direction")

  4. At the horizon, the difference between the pull on the feet and on the head reaches about 19 million times Earth's gravity.

    our calculation: 1.9 × 10⁷ g for 1.8 m (2.1 × 10⁷ g for 2 m)calc

  5. So near a stellar black hole, a body is torn apart long before it reaches the edge.

    Spaghettification, Wikipedia

  6. Stephen Hawking described this as being "stretched like spaghetti" (A Brief History of Time, 1988); today it is called spaghettification. He popularised the image; he did not coin it.

    Spaghettification, Wikipedia (also cites Nigel Calder, 1977)

  7. The way to survive the crossing is a much bigger black hole: a hole ten times heavier has an edge ten times wider, where the stretch is a hundred times weaker.

    Schwarzschild radius, Wikipedia (radius grows with mass)·our calculation: the horizon tide falls as 1/M²calc

  8. Small black holes curve space much more sharply at their edge than big ones (the beach ball and the planet).

    Spaghettification, Wikipedia ("much higher spacetime curvature at their event horizon")

  9. M87*, the black hole in the first image, weighs 6.5 billion Suns.

    eso1907, ESO / EHT, 2019

  10. At M87*'s edge the stretch is far too small to feel (about 4.5 × 10⁻¹¹ g).

    our calculationcalc·Spaghettification, Wikipedia (supermassive: crossed "without noticing any significant tidal forces")

  11. Falling in, the darkness below grows as the sky's light crowds into a smaller patch.

    Falling into a black hole: simulations, Andrew Hamilton, JILA (standard relativity; we found no quotable text, see caveats)

  12. At the horizon there is no wall, no flash, and no sign that you have crossed.

    Inside black holes, Andrew Hamilton, JILA ("you would not see any sign that you crossed the horizon")

  13. The sky above does not go dark as you cross.

    Inside black holes, Andrew Hamilton, JILA ("the view of the outside Universe will not disappear")

2:59

Time at the Edge

  1. Gravity slows clocks: a clock held still near a mass ticks slower than one far away.

    Gravitational time dilation, Wikipedia

  2. The popular story that you would see the universe's whole future flash past is wrong for someone falling freely into a black hole that doesn't spin: light from the far future can't catch up before the fall ends.

    What happens to you if you fall into a black hole?, Usenet Physics FAQ (John Baez et al., UC Riverside), no date·Is it possible to see the infinite future of the Universe when falling into a black hole?, Grib & Pavlov, arXiv, 2009

  3. The fast-forward is real only for someone hovering just above the edge on rockets.

    Gravitational time dilation, Wikipedia (the static-clock formula)

  4. Near M87*, one hour of hovering could be 7 years for everyone else, but holding still there takes about 15 million g of thrust.

    our calculation: a factor of 61,362 needs 1.5 × 10⁷ g at M87*calc·formula: Gravitational time dilation, Wikipedia

  5. A friend watching from far away sees you slow down near the edge, turn red and dim, and appear to freeze.

    Usenet Physics FAQ, Baez et al.·Inside black holes, Hamilton, JILA ("dim and redshifted")

  6. The frozen image doesn't last: around M87* it fades to black on a timescale of days.

    our derivation: the light dims by a factor e about every 36 hours at M87*calc·Usenet Physics FAQ ("things would wink out as they got close")

  7. For the one falling there is no freeze at all, and the drop from the edge of M87* to the centre lasts a little over a day at most (27.9 hours).

    Schwarzschild geodesics, Wikipedia (formula)·our calculation: πGM/c³calc

  8. The spaghetti stretch catches up only in the last tenth of a second, about as long as a blink.

    Inside black holes, Hamilton, JILA ("approximately a tenth of a second before you hit the singularity")

  9. What waits at the very centre, nobody knows: it is where general relativity breaks down.

    Gravitational singularity, Wikipedia

  10. Real black holes like M87* spin, and inside spinning black holes things get even stranger.

    Usenet Physics FAQ, Baez et al. (rotating and charged holes)·Gravitational singularity, Wikipedia

Simplifications and honest caveats

What we left out, what is uncertain, and where the numbers depend on choices we made.

  • Simplified

    Our black holes don't spin

    Every number in the film is for the simplest, non-spinning (Schwarzschild) black hole. Real ones like M87* spin, and inside a spinning hole there is a second, inner horizon whose fate is unknown. The "no fast-forward" result and the fall times are for the non-spinning case only.

  • Simplified

    The numbers assume a 1.8 m person

    Tidal stretch grows with body length: 19 million g for 1.8 m becomes 21 million g for 2 m. "10 Suns" is a working value for a typical stellar black hole; NASA's range runs from three to dozens of Suns.

  • Simplified

    The picture is not the black hole

    The 2019 image shows glowing gas around a shadow. The shadow is about 2.5 times wider than the event horizon, which can never be seen directly.

  • Uncertain

    "Fades in days" is our derivation

    No source states a fade time for M87*. We derived it from how fast light from near the horizon redshifts (one e-fold every 4GM/c³, about 36 hours). Read it as an order of magnitude.

  • Uncertain

    The growing darkness below

    That the sky crowds into a shrinking bright patch as you fall is standard relativity, shown in Andrew Hamilton's simulations, but we found no quotable text for it, so the film keeps it to the picture and gives no numbers.

  • Uncertain

    The lightest black hole

    GW230529's heavier object (about 2.5 to 4.5 Suns) might be a heavy neutron star; even its discoverers did not call it certain. That is why the film says "none confirmed below about 3 Suns".

  • Unknown

    The centre

    General relativity predicts a singularity, but nobody knows what really happens there; a theory of quantum gravity is probably needed. Whether singularities exist at all in real black holes is open.

  • Uncertain

    Fall times don't all agree

    Hamilton quotes 16 seconds from horizon to centre for a hole of about 5 million Suns on his simulated path; our formula gives 33 to 77 seconds. We use the formula's maximum (the longest possible fall) in the film.

  • Simplified

    Hovering is a thought experiment

    Fifteen million g would crush any person or rocket. "One hour, seven years" is a real ratio of clock rates, not something anyone could do. (Interstellar's version uses a fast-spinning hole and an orbit, not hovering.)

  • How we know

    Some sources are secondary

    Several entries are Wikipedia pages, used for textbook formulas whose numbers we recomputed. Quotes reached us through a page-reading tool, so check punctuation on the original pages. The London to Beijing distance is general knowledge and not in our research notes.
