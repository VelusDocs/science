What Explodes After the Last Star Dies?

A black dwarf is a star that has cooled to nothing, and it has no heat left to explode with. One physicist calculated in 2020 that, if protons last forever, the heaviest ones could still blow up after about 10¹¹⁰⁰ years. This page lists every claim the film makes, where each one comes from, and which parts are one calculation and which are plain assumption.

A black ball in the dark

  1. A black dwarf is the cold, dark remains of a star, almost as cold as anything in the universe can get.

    Black dwarf, Wikipedia·"almost as cold as anything" is our phrasing of the cooling limit

  2. In that far future there are no stars left in its sky and nobody around to see it.

    Adams & Laughlin 1997 (star formation ends by about 10¹⁴ years)·"nobody around" is our inference, not a finding

  3. In 2020 a physicist calculated that a dead white dwarf could one day explode, and such explosions may be the last supernovas the universe ever has.

    Black dwarf supernova in the far future, M. E. Caplan, MNRAS 497, 4357, 2020·Illinois State University release, 11 Aug 2020 ("They may be the last supernova ever")·one physicist's calculation, not an observation

  4. A dead star has no heat left to set off fusion, which is why the explosion is a puzzle.

    Caplan 2020 (fusion without thermal energy)·fusion needing heat is standard physics

  5. The wait is about 10¹¹⁰⁰ years: you would have to say the word "trillion" about 92 times to name it.

    Caplan 2020·Illinois State University release, 11 Aug 2020 ("almost a hundred")·our calculation: 1100 ÷ 12 = 91.7calc

A star without a fire

  1. In about five billion years the Sun runs out of hydrogen at its centre, swells into a red giant and sheds its outer layers, leaving a hot core of carbon and oxygen: a white dwarf.

    The fate of the Sun, Windows to the Universe (NASA-funded)

  2. That white dwarf holds about half the Sun's mass in a ball the size of Earth.

    Adams & Laughlin 1997 (about 0.5 Suns)·the usual 0.5 to 0.6 range; our research found no primary quote for it

  3. Electrons squeezed this tightly resist further squeezing and hold the star up, up to about 1.4 Suns: the Chandrasekhar limit.

    Caplan 2020 (maximum mass ≈ 1.44 (2Yₑ)² Suns)·the electron explanation is standard background physics; the practical value is about 1.4 (the ideal formula gives 1.457)

  4. Over time a white dwarf fades until it gives off no light. That is a black dwarf, and it has nothing to do with a black hole.

    Black dwarf, Wikipedia

  5. No black dwarf exists yet, because the universe (13.8 billion years old) is not old enough.

    Black dwarf, Wikipedia

  6. One of the coolest white dwarfs known, WD J2147−4035, has cooled for about 10 billion years and its surface (about 3,050 K) is still more than half as hot as the Sun's (5,772 K).

    Elms et al., MNRAS 517, 4557, 2022 (temperature "about 3050 K", cooling age "over 10 Gyr")·our calculation: 3048 ÷ 5772 = 0.53calc

  7. A white dwarf needs at least a hundred trillion years to cool fully, thousands of times the age of the universe.

    Black dwarf, Wikipedia, citing Barrow & Tipler 1986 ("10¹⁵ years" to reach 5 K; we could not open the book)·our calculation: 10¹⁴ ÷ 1.38 × 10¹⁰ ≈ 7,200calc

A dying universe

  1. Astronomers have mapped the universe's future in eras; we live in the first long one, the age of stars.

    A Dying Universe, Adams & Laughlin, Rev. Mod. Phys. 69, 337, 1997

  2. The big bright stars burn out first, then new stars stop forming; the last small red star fades around a hundred trillion years from now.

    Adams & Laughlin 1997 (star formation essentially over by η ≈ 14, i.e. 10¹⁴ years)·our reading: the last star is born near 10¹⁴ yearscalc

  3. In the degenerate era, white dwarfs cool into black dwarfs.

    Adams & Laughlin 1997 (their framework assumes protons decay; see the caveats)·Black dwarf, Wikipedia

  4. Many physicists suspect protons eventually fall apart. Nobody has seen it, and the explosion only happens if they never do.

    Adams & Laughlin 1997 (proton lifetime between 10³² and 10⁴⁹ years: "the upper bound is more suggestive than definitive")·Caplan 2020

  5. Hawking radiation would slowly dissolve even black holes; the biggest are gone after about a hundred zeros' worth of years, though nobody has observed it.

    Adams & Laughlin 1997 (η ≈ 98 for a galaxy-mass hole)·Caplan 2020 ("10¹⁰⁰ years")·our check: 10⁸³ × (10⁵)³ = 10⁹⁸calc

  6. After that comes the dark era, close to what people call heat death.

    Adams & Laughlin 1997 (not strictly identical to heat death, so the film says "close to")

  7. If protons never decay, the black dwarfs are still out there in the dark.

    assumption: the film's premise, not an established factassumption

  8. In 2020 Matt Caplan asked what happens to them if you simply keep waiting.

    Black dwarf supernova in the far future, M. E. Caplan, MNRAS 497, 4357, 2020

4:16

Fusion without fire

  1. Fusion powers the Sun and normally needs enormous heat, because nuclei repel each other; a black dwarf has no heat.

    standard physics·Caplan 2020

  2. Inside a black dwarf the nuclei are locked in a crystal, squeezed against neighbours and trembling in place.

    Black dwarf supernova in the far future, M. E. Caplan, MNRAS 497, 4357, 2020 ("on the crystal lattice")

  3. Quantum tunnelling gives a nucleus a tiny chance of turning up on the far side of the repulsive wall.

    Black dwarf supernova in the far future, M. E. Caplan, MNRAS 497, 4357, 2020 ("driven by quantum tunneling of adjacent nuclei on the crystal lattice")·APS DNP 2020 abstract

  4. This is pycnonuclear fusion: density does the job that heat does in the Sun.

    Caplan 2020·Black dwarf, Wikipedia

  5. Fusion at zero temperature is slow but real, and the star slowly turns toward iron.

    Illinois State University release, 11 Aug 2020 (Caplan: "Fusion happens, even at zero temperature, it just takes a really long time")·Caplan 2020 (iron-56). At collapse the star is only partly iron, so the film says "toward iron"

  6. By this calculation a black dwarf is still fusing, only far too slowly to see.

    Caplan 2020·theory extrapolated to near-zero temperature; the rates have never been measuredone study

Say trillion ninety-two times

  1. Caplan calculated that the heaviest black dwarfs need about 10¹¹⁰⁰ years before anything dramatic happens.

    Black dwarf supernova in the far future, M. E. Caplan, MNRAS 497, 4357, 2020 (1.35 Suns; "10¹¹⁰⁰ years"; exponents "rounded to the nearest hundred")·one author's estimate, the exponent is coarseone study

  2. That is a one followed by eleven hundred zeros.

    by definition of 10¹¹⁰⁰calc

  3. Dividing that wait by the universe's whole age so far removes only about ten zeros.

    our calculation: 13.8 × 10⁹ = 10¹⁰·¹⁴calc

  4. If protons do fall apart, black dwarfs evaporate within about 10³² to 10⁴⁹ years and the explosion never happens. Some theories of physics predict proton decay.

    Adams & Laughlin 1997 ("the upper bound is more suggestive than definitive")·Caplan 2020·the film says "no more than fifty zeros"; 10⁴⁹ is a suggestive upper end, not a hard cap, and proton decay could be slower

  5. Experiments have looked for a dying proton and found none: protons live longer than a number with thirty-four zeros, which is nothing next to eleven hundred.

    Super-Kamiokande proton decay search, Phys. Rev. D 102, 112011, 2020 (τ/B > 2.4 × 10³⁴ years for p → e⁺π⁰ and > 1.6 × 10³⁴ for p → μ⁺π⁰, 90% confidence)·applies to the decay modes searched

  6. Nobody knows whether protons last forever.

    Super-Kamiokande proton decay search, Phys. Rev. D 102, 112011, 2020·Adams & Laughlin 1997

  7. The explosion is still only one physicist's calculation, nobody has observed any of it, and as far as we can find nobody has repeated it independently.

    Caplan 2020 ("few observable consequences")·our literature search found no independent recalculation; absence of evidence, not proof

  8. A ball of iron has no reason to explode; you could turn the whole Sun to iron and it would just sit there.

    Illinois State University release, 11 Aug 2020 (Caplan, "You could turn the whole sun to iron…")·Caplan 2020

7:29

The limit comes down

  1. The Chandrasekhar limit depends on how many electrons a star has for its weight, and iron carries fewer.

    Caplan 2020 (maximum mass scales as Yₑ²)

  2. Iron-56 has 26 protons in 56 nucleons, so 0.464 electrons per nucleon instead of 0.5.

    Caplan 2020 ("Yₑ = 26/56 = 0.464")·our check: 26 ÷ 56 = 0.464calc

  3. As matter turns to iron the star loses the electrons holding it up, and the limit sinks from about 1.4 toward about 1.2 Suns.

    Caplan 2020 ("smaller by a factor (0.464/0.5)² ≈ 0.86")·our check: 1.4 × 0.86 ≈ 1.2 (ideal formula 1.257)calc·simplified: the paper's own collapse trigger is lower (about 1.12, from electron capture), and the loss of electrons is really Ni-56 positron decay

  4. Our Sun's black dwarf, far lighter, is not one of the stars that explode.

    Caplan 2020 (progenitors between about 1.16 and 1.35 Suns)·Adams & Laughlin 1997 (a typical white dwarf is about 0.5 Suns)

  5. The heaviest black dwarfs already weigh close to the limit, so the limit reaches them first: about 10¹¹⁰⁰ years for 1.35 Suns, 10¹⁶⁰⁰ for 1.24, 10³²⁰⁰⁰ for 1.16.

    Black dwarf supernova in the far future, M. E. Caplan, MNRAS 497, 4357, 2020·the 10³²⁰⁰⁰ figure comes with the author's warning that "at such low densities this treatment may not be appropriate"

  6. When the electrons can no longer hold the star up, it collapses under its own weight and explodes.

    Caplan 2020 (resembling accretion-induced collapse of O/Ne/Mg white dwarfs; the lighter cases resemble stripped-envelope core-collapse supernovae)·a prediction, not an observationone study

  7. Nobody has published a simulation of one of these explosions, so nobody knows how bright they would be, or whether every collapse really ends in a blast.

    Caplan 2020 (the outcome needs simulations)·our search found no follow-up

  8. Only about one star in a hundred ends up heavy enough: roughly a billion trillion stars in the observable universe.

    Caplan 2020 ("approximately one percent of all stars today, about 10²¹ stars")·Illinois State University release, 11 Aug 2020 ("a billion trillion")·assumes about 10²³ stars in total, itself uncertain by a large factor

  9. The last of them may wait for about thirty-two thousand zeros' worth of years.

    Caplan 2020 ("lasting no more than about 10³²⁰⁰⁰ years")

  10. As far as anyone can tell, there is nothing out there to see the light.

    our inference; the research notes mark this as nuanced, not establishedassumption

  11. We are living inside those first ten zeros, while the sky is still full of stars.

    our calculation: 13.8 × 10⁹ years ≈ 10¹⁰calc·figurative

  12. Almost certainly nobody will be there to watch the last explosions.

    opinion, softened by the fact-check from "nobody"

Simplifications and honest caveats

What we left out, what is uncertain, and what the whole story depends on.

  • Uncertain

    One physicist's calculation

    Everything from 10¹¹⁰⁰ years onward rests on one 2020 paper by Matt Caplan. We found no independent recalculation and no simulation of the collapse. Nobody has observed any part of it, and the paper itself says it has "few observable consequences". The exponent is rounded to the nearest hundred and could shift by many orders.

  • Assumption

    Protons must never decay

    The explosion only happens if protons last effectively forever. If they decay, black dwarfs evaporate in 10³² to 10⁴⁹ years, long before 10¹¹⁰⁰, and the film's event never occurs. Super-Kamiokande has found no decay (lifetime above about 10³⁴ years for the modes it searched), but that rules out almost nothing next to 10¹¹⁰⁰. The film says "no more than fifty zeros"; Adams and Laughlin call 10⁴⁹ "more suggestive than definitive", so read it as a typical figure, not a hard limit.

  • Uncertain

    A rival limit the film does not mention

    In 2025 Falcke, Wondrak and van Suijlekom argued that stellar remnants decay through gravitational pair production even with stable protons. Their only worked white dwarf example (1.3 Suns) lasts about 10⁷⁸ years, which would end black dwarfs about 10¹⁰²² times before Caplan's explosion. Their 2023 foundation paper was formally challenged and defended in the same journal, and we found no independent confirmation of either side. We checked this after the script was locked and the owner chose to leave the film unchanged, so it is listed here instead. If they are right, Caplan's explosion does not happen; if they are wrong, it stands, still subject to the proton assumption.

  • Uncertain

    Fusion rates were never measured

    Pycnonuclear rates at near-zero temperature and crystal densities are extrapolated theory. The last figure (10³²⁰⁰⁰ years) comes from a regime the author says may be outside his treatment.

  • Uncertain

    What the collapse looks like

    It might be an explosion, a collapse into a neutron star, or a fizzle. No simulation exists, so the film makes no claim about brightness.

  • Simplified

    "Turns to iron" and "loses electrons"

    At collapse the star is only partly iron, and the heavy black dwarfs are mostly oxygen, neon and magnesium rather than carbon and oxygen. The limit falls toward about 1.2 Suns in the simple formula, while the paper's own trigger is lower still (about 1.12 Suns, through electron capture).

  • Simplified

    "Heat death" and the eras

    The era timeline is one framework (Adams and Laughlin, 1997), and it assumes protons decay. The dark era is close to heat death, not strictly the same thing. Hawking radiation has never been observed either.

  • Uncertain

    How long a white dwarf takes to cool

    The film says "at least a hundred trillion years". Barrow and Tipler give about a quadrillion years to reach 5 K (we did not open the book), and dark matter might keep some dwarfs warm for much longer. WD J2147−4035's cooling age is model-dependent ("over 10 Gyr"), and the paper's claim covers its own class, not every white dwarf.

  • Uncertain

    The "billion trillion" count

    It is Caplan's estimate of one percent of stars. The total number of stars in the observable universe is itself uncertain by a factor of a few to hundreds, so treat it as an order of magnitude.

  • How we know

    Some sources are secondary

    Wikipedia's Black dwarf page and the university press release are secondary; we used them for context and read the numbers against the papers. Claims marked "calc" are our own arithmetic, rechecked by a separate fact-checker. The 2020 paper is linked through its DOI (MNRAS); the free arXiv copy is arXiv:2008.02296.
