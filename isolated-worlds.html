Small Worlds Make Strange Things

Islands, caves and old lakes shut life in for thousands of years, and life starts writing its own rules: elephants shrink, fish throw away their eyes, and an axolotl refuses to grow up. Then somebody comes. This page lists every claim the film makes, with its source, and says plainly what is a model, what is contested and what is a drawing.

What the film claims, and where it comes from

In film order, one or two sentences per line. The locked door, the NO VISITORS sign and the glowing DNA ribbons are metaphors and drawings; the claims under them are not. Chapter times follow the current voice track and may shift by a few seconds. Where a publisher blocks automated readers, we confirmed that the link resolves and that the paper's title and abstract match in an open index; the notes say which sentences we could not check against a source.

0:00

Hook

  1. Once, on the island of Sicily, there lived an elephant about as heavy as a big pig.

    Köhler et al., Scientific Reports, 2021 (the Sicilian dwarf elephant Palaeoloxodon falconeri stood about 1 m tall)·Romano et al., Historical Biology, 2019 (volumetric estimate for one cave sample: about 250 kg for an adult male, about 150 kg for an adult female)

  2. In the caves under the Balkans, a salamander may live past a hundred while barely moving from its spot.

    Voituron et al., Biology Letters, 2010 (predicted maximum lifespan over 100 years)·Balázs et al., Journal of Zoology, 2020 (the olm is sedentary, often within a few square metres for years)

  3. In the canals of Mexico City, an animal spends its whole life refusing to grow up.

    Monaghan et al., Regeneration, 2014 (axolotls are "normally paedomorphic": they stay in the larval body form as adults)·Conservation International (the Xochimilco canals are the only wild home of the axolotl)

  4. They come from small worlds, islands, caves and old lakes, where life got shut in for thousands of years or far longer.

    Summary of the three small worlds below, with their sources: islands (Sicily, Flores), caves (the Mexican tetra, the olm) and old lakes (Baikal, Victoria, Xochimilco)

  5. Behind that door, life starts writing its own rules: elephants shrink, fish throw away their eyes, and children refuse to leave home.

    Summary of the elephant, cavefish and axolotl claims below. "Throw away" and "children" are figures of speech; the door and the NO VISITORS sign are a metaphor, not a fact

  6. But the same door that makes these worlds so strange also leaves them dangerously easy to break.

    Summary of the Guam, moa, Lake Victoria and Xochimilco claims in the last chapter

1:04

The island that shrinks elephants

  1. Again and again in the deep past, elephants ended up on islands, and again and again, they shrank.

    van der Geer et al., Journal of Biogeography, 2016 (22 insular fossil elephant species on 17 islands: "all but one" became dwarfs)

  2. On Sicily, their ancestor was a straight-tusked giant about four metres tall at the shoulder.

    Palaeoloxodon antiquus, Wikipedia (mature bulls averaged 4 m at the shoulder). A general reference, not a primary paper; see the notes

  3. Its island descendants stood about a metre tall at the shoulder. An elephant you could pat on the head.

    Köhler et al., 2021 ("The 1-m-tall dwarf elephant")

  4. You might expect a tiny elephant to be a sort of permanent baby. It wasn't: it still took about fifteen years to grow up and lived for decades, just like its giant relatives.

    Köhler et al., 2021 (bone, molar and tusk histology: very slow growth, maturity at about 15 years, a minimum lifespan of 68 years)

  5. On Flores, an island in Indonesia, the local rats grew into giants.

    Locatelli et al., Quaternary International, 2012 (the giant rats of Liang Bua are "a clear example of insular gigantism")

  6. This pull has a name, the island rule: on islands, big animals tend to shrink and small ones tend to grow.

    Benítez-López et al., Nature Ecology & Evolution, 2021 (a meta-analysis of island and mainland populations of land vertebrates)·Foster, Nature, 1964 (the early comparison of island and mainland mammals)

  7. It works on mammals, birds and reptiles alike, and for mammals and reptiles it pulls hardest on small, remote islands.

    Benítez-López et al., 2021 (effects "widespread in mammals, birds and reptiles"; "more pronounced effects in smaller, more remote islands for mammals and reptiles")

  8. Nobody fully knows why, and plenty of islanders simply ignore it.

    Meiri, Raia & Phillimore, Journal of Biogeography, 2010 ("Slaying dragons: limited evidence for unusual body size evolution on islands")

  9. Between about a hundred thousand and sixty thousand years ago, Flores was also home to a small human relative with a small brain, nicknamed the hobbit.

    Sutikna et al., Nature, 2016 (skeletal remains of Homo floresiensis dated to about 100 to 60 thousand years ago; stone artefacts about 190 to 50 thousand)

  10. And teeth from the same island, about seven hundred thousand years old, are already about as small.

    van den Bergh et al., Nature, 2016 (a jaw fragment and isolated teeth from Mata Menge, 700,000 years old, "as small as, or smaller than" those from Liang Bua)

  11. Did an older human shrink on Flores, the way the elephants shrank on Sicily? Maybe, but we don't know yet.

    van den Bergh et al., 2016 (the fossils fit descent from Asian Homo erectus, but the origin of the hobbit "has been intensively debated")

2:50

Travel light in the dark

  1. In Mexico, a little fish called the Mexican tetra lives a double life: in the rivers it has eyes, and in the caves it's blind.

    Yamamoto & Jeffery, Science, 2000 (Astyanax mexicanus has an eyed surface form and an eyeless cave form)

  2. A cavefish embryo still packs its eyes for the trip: it starts building them, lens and all. Then the lens cells die, and the half-built eye is taken apart.

    Yamamoto & Jeffery, 2000 ("Eye formation is initiated in cave fish embryos, but the eye subsequently arrests and degenerates")·Yamamoto et al., Nature, 2004 (Hedgehog signalling at the embryonic midline controls the eye degeneration)·Hinaux et al., PLOS ONE, 2017 (lens cell death in the cavefish embryo)

  3. Everything hangs on that lens: swap in a lens from a river cousin, and the cavefish's eye keeps on growing.

    Yamamoto & Jeffery, 2000 (a surface fish lens transplanted into a cave fish optic cup stimulates growth and restores optic tissues)

  4. In the dark, eyes are heavy luggage: they cost energy to build and to run, and some biologists think saving that energy helped, alongside slow neglect.

    Moran et al., Science Advances, 2015 (the eyes and optic tectum cost about 15% of resting metabolism in a 1 g eyed fish)·Protas et al., Current Biology, 2007 (cave alleles at every eye QTL shrink the eye, "consistent with evolution by natural selection but not with drift")

  5. The packing may have been quick: somewhere between a few million years and, if the newest, still argued dates hold, less than twenty thousand years.

    Fumey et al., BMC Evolutionary Biology, 2018 (microsatellite data "strongly support a very recent origin of cave populations"; older estimates reach millions of years)

  6. In the caves under the Balkans lives the ultimate homebody, the olm, a cave salamander. A tagged olm seemed to stay in the same spot for seven years.

    Balázs et al., Journal of Zoology, 2020 (an eight-year capture-mark-recapture study; the olm is sedentary and "can often be found within a few square metres over several years")·Science News (one olm "appeared to haunt roughly the same spot for seven years")

  7. Olms watched in a French cave lab for decades look set to live past a hundred. That's a prediction, not a birthday anyone has actually seen.

    Voituron et al., Biology Letters, 2010 (a captive colony recorded since 1958: "a predicted maximum lifespan of over 100 years", an adult average of 68.5 years, first breeding at 15.6 years)

  8. With nothing to eat, an olm can last at least a year. First it burns its sugar, then its fat, then fat and protein, while slowing itself down almost to a stop.

    Hervant, Mathieu & Durand, Journal of Experimental Biology, 2001 (starving olms went through "successive periods of glucidic, lipidic and finally lipido-proteic-dominant catabolism", with "protein sparing and temporary torpor"). Our copy of the abstract does not give the length of the fast; see the notes

4:57

The lakes that play with time

  1. The oldest lake of all, Lake Baikal in Siberia, is about twenty-five million years old.

    Lake Baikal, IUCN World Heritage Outlook ("the oldest (25 million years)")

  2. It has been a lake about eighty times longer than our species has existed.

    Our arithmetic: 25 million years divided by about 300,000 years is about 83. The age of our species comes from Hublin et al., Nature, 2017 (the earliest Homo sapiens fossils, 300,000 to 350,000 years old)

  3. Most of the species living in it are found nowhere else on Earth, and among them is a seal.

    Lake Baikal, IUCN (at least 2,640 animal species, about 56% endemic; other sources give higher shares)

  4. It is the only seal species on Earth that spends its entire life in fresh water.

    Baikal seal, Pinniped Conservation ("the only pinniped species that lives solely in freshwater")

  5. Probably its ancestors, ringed seals from the north, swam up rivers during an ice age. When and how is still argued.

    Sasaki et al., Zoological Science, 2003 (the Baikal seal is "much more closely related to the ringed seal"; an ancestor came down to the lake about 0.4 million years ago)·Pinniped Conservation ("the most accepted theory suggests descent from the ringed seal"; the origin is uncertain). The rivers-and-ice-age route is not in the sources we could open

  6. Lake Victoria, in East Africa, plays with time the other way: its basin may have dried out less than fifteen thousand years ago.

    Johnson et al., Science, 1996 (the lake "dried up completely during the Late Pleistocene")·Nagl et al., Proceedings of the Royal Society B, 2000 (geology "suggests that the LV basin dried out less than 15,000 years ago")

  7. Yet today it's home to more than five hundred species of cichlids, small fish with a talent for turning into new species.

    Kishe-Machumu et al., Journal of Great Lakes Research, 2015 (a fauna "dominated by 500+ species of haplochromines"; the count is an estimate)

  8. If the whole flock really is that young, that's a new species every thirty years or so, roughly a species per human generation.

    Our arithmetic: 15,000 years divided by 500 species is 30 years per species, only if the whole flock arose after the dry-up. Nagl et al. (above) note that molecular dating gave 250,000 to 750,000 years

  9. Our last lake sits in the middle of Mexico City. What's left of an old lake survives there as a maze of canals, dug between farm islands since Aztec times. It's the only place on Earth where axolotls live in the wild.

    Conservation International ("an ancient wetland system of islands and canals that pre-dates the Aztecs"; "the only place on Earth where you can find" the axolotl; the farm islands are called chinampas)

  10. Most salamanders start as swimming larvae with gills, then transform into adults and walk out onto land. The axolotl skips that part: it keeps its gills, stays in the water and has babies as a larva, a trick called neoteny.

    Monaghan et al., Regeneration, 2014 ("normally paedomorphic adult axolotls" in contrast to metamorphic salamanders)

  11. Give an axolotl a dose of thyroid hormone, and it will transform after all. But it pays for the trip: in a study, its famous power to regrow a lost limb slowed to about half speed.

    Monaghan et al., 2014 (induced metamorphosis by thyroxine "causes a twofold reduction in regeneration rate")

  12. Why stay young? Perhaps a calm, permanent lake never gave it a reason to leave, but nobody has proven that.

    A hypothesis, said as one in the film. Our research found no primary source that tests it

7:25

And then somebody came

  1. After the Second World War, a snake reached Guam, an island in the Pacific, probably hidden in military cargo.

    Brown Treesnake Project, USGS ("accidentally introduced to Guam from the Admiralty Islands probably soon after the end of World War II")·Brown treesnake, USDA APHIS Wildlife Services (introduced in the late 1940s or early 1950s). Neither page says "cargo"; see the notes

  2. Over the next decades, it spread across the island, and wherever it spread, the forest birds disappeared.

    Savidge, Ecology, 1987 ("The range expansion of B. irregularis correlated well with the range contraction of the forest avifauna")

  3. Today, most of Guam's native forest birds are gone.

    Wiles et al., Conservation Biology, 2003 (snakes caused "the extirpation or serious reduction of most of the island's 25 resident bird species")·USDA APHIS ("the extirpation or extinction of most of Guam's native forest birds and lizards")

  4. The snakes climb power lines, and in the twenty years to 1997, they caused more than sixteen hundred blackouts.

    Fritts, International Biodeterioration & Biodegradation, 2002, USGS record ("more than 1600 power outages" in the 20 years from 1978 to 1997)

  5. In New Zealand, the visitors were us. Its moa were big flightless birds, and their DNA shows large, stable populations right up until people arrived.

    Allentoft et al., PNAS, 2014 (ancient DNA and radiocarbon dates from 281 moa: "not in decline before human colonization")

  6. The first settlers probably never numbered more than about two thousand, roughly the size of a large high school. In under a hundred and fifty years, by hunting and clearing the land, they pushed the moa beyond saving.

    Holdaway et al., Nature Communications, 2014 (the Polynesian population "would not have exceeded 2,000 individuals"; a brief "<150 years" period of hunting and habitat removal). The high-school comparison is ours

  7. In the fifties and sixties, people released the Nile perch into Lake Victoria, a fish that eats other fish.

    Pringle, BioScience, 2005 (repeated secretive introductions in the mid-1950s)·Witte et al., 2013 (the Nile perch was introduced in the 1950s)

  8. By the early eighties, the perch were booming and the cichlids were crashing.

    Goldschmidt, Witte & Wanink, Conservation Biology, 1993 ("In the 1980s an explosive increase of the introduced Nile perch")·Kishe-Machumu et al., 2015 (the sub-littoral haplochromines "vanished almost completely" in the 1980s)

  9. By different counts, somewhere between forty and sixty-five percent of the cichlid species vanished.

    Goldschmidt et al., 1993 ("approximately 65%")·Witte et al., 2013 ("some 200 of the endemic haplochromine species may have gone extinct", out of about 500, which is about 40%)·Witte et al., Environmental Biology of Fishes, 1992

  10. But the perch didn't do it alone. The lake was also turning murky and short of oxygen, and fishing nets were dragging along its bottom.

    Kishe-Machumu et al., 2015 (algal blooms, lower oxygen and lower water transparency in the 1980s, with months of hypoxia in the Mwanza Gulf)·Witte et al., 2013 (the haplochromine decline in trawl catches from 1973 to 1984 "was mainly due to trawl fishery"; the later decline was caused by the perch upsurge)

  11. Some species have since crept back.

    Kishe-Machumu et al., 2015 ("a recovery of some haplochromine species was observed" from the 1990s)

  12. Behind the locked door, life had time alone, enough to shrink an elephant or unpack an eye. Open that door, and a single visitor can undo in decades what took thousands of years to build.

    Closing summary of the claims above. The door is a metaphor

  13. Back in the canals of Mexico City, the visitors were carp and tilapia, brought in from the seventies on as food fish.

    Creating refuges for axolotls in Mexico City, The Wildlife Society ("Carp and tilapia were introduced in the 1970s as a food source for residents")

  14. They eat axolotl eggs, and they eat the axolotl's dinner. Pollution and a spreading city do the rest.

    The Wildlife Society ("carp will feed on axolotl eggs and tilapia prey on juvenile salamanders")·Conservation International (habitat loss, pollution, and carp and tilapia, which prey on axolotls and compete with them)

  15. At the end of the nineties, each square kilometre of canal held about six thousand axolotls. That's around forty of them in every football pitch of water.

    Conservation International (6,000 per square kilometre in 1998, from UNAM surveys). Football pitches are our arithmetic: 6,000 divided by about 140 pitches per square kilometre

  16. By the mid twenty-tens, it was thirty-six. An axolotl for every four football pitches.

    Conservation International (36 per square kilometre in 2014, "a 99 percent drop"). 36 divided by about 140 pitches is about one per four

  17. More recently, people searched the canals at a hundred and fifteen sites. They didn't catch a single axolotl.

    Conservation International (115 monitoring sites; "Although no axolotls were captured with the nets")

  18. But the water itself still carries their DNA, tiny traces they shed as they swim. They're still in there.

    Conservation International (eDNA from water samples "revealed axolotls are still present in the canals"). The glowing ribbons in the film are a drawing, not what eDNA looks like

  19. Still young, still home, and still hoping for no visitors.

    Figure of speech, not a claim

Simplifications and honest caveats

Ten minutes is short. Here is what we left out, simplified or could not confirm.

  • Uncertain

    The olm's hundred years is a prediction

    Voituron's team recorded a captive colony (400+ animals, weekly since 1958) and used a model to predict a maximum lifespan of over 100 years, an average adult lifespan of 68.5 years and first breeding at 15.6 years. Captive animals are fed more than wild ones, and nobody has watched an olm reach a hundred. The film says "look set to" and "a prediction".

  • Uncertain

    How long an olm can fast

    The 2001 study shows how olms burn their fuel during long fasting (sugar, then fat, then fat and protein) and that they stay in a state of protein sparing and torpor. We could not read the length of the fast in the copy of the paper we could open, so the film's "at least a year" rests on our research notes, which cite a secondary report. Treat it as unconfirmed here. A figure of ten years circulates online and has no primary source, so the film does not use it.

  • Simplified

    One olm, seven years

    The seven years is one animal in an eight-year capture-mark-recapture study of a cave in Herzegovina, found at the same spot by sight, with no record of where it went in between. "Barely moving" is the study's own finding for the others too: most stayed within a few square metres to a few metres for years.

  • Uncertain

    How old cavefish blindness is

    The newest analysis points to a very recent origin of the cave populations, under 20,000 years, but it comes from one kind of genetic data and a few caves. Older estimates reach millions of years. Eye loss was not simply disuse either: genetic mapping fits natural selection, and the eye is costly to run, so "alongside slow neglect" is as far as the evidence goes.

  • Simplified

    Cavefish: the lens is not the whole story

    Lens cell death is the main driver of the eye's breakdown, and a river-fish lens rescues the eye in a transplant. A small lens alone does not trigger the death, though (Hinaux et al., 2017), and the signal that starts it is Hedgehog at the embryo's midline. The film keeps only the lens.

  • Simplified

    The elephant's weight and its ancestor

    "About as heavy as a big pig" is our reading of body-mass estimates of about 150 to 250 kg for the Sicilian adults in one cave sample, and other estimates differ. The four-metre ancestor is a mean for mature bulls from a general reference, not from a paper we could open. An 8-tonne figure in an earlier draft was cut because we could not source it.

  • Uncertain

    The hobbit's origin

    The bones are dated to about 100 to 60 thousand years ago, and the older Mata Menge teeth are as small or smaller. Whether the hobbit was a shrunken Homo erectus or came from an older, smaller lineage is still debated. That is why the film says "maybe, but we don't know yet".

  • Simplified

    The island rule is a trend, not a law

    The 2021 meta-analysis finds the pattern widespread in mammals, birds and reptiles but weaker in amphibians, and strongest on small, remote islands for mammals and reptiles only. Others find limited evidence for it. The film says "tend to" and "plenty of islanders simply ignore it".

  • Simplified

    "Most" Baikal species are endemic

    The IUCN lists at least 2,640 animal species, about 56% endemic. A popular source gives 80% of more than 3,700 species, a broader count that we could not open. "Most" holds either way. The route of the seal's ancestors by river and ice age is the usual idea; we found no source that settles it.

  • Uncertain

    Victoria's thirty-year species

    The basin dried out less than 15,000 years ago on the geological evidence, but DNA dating of the cichlids gives 250,000 to 750,000 years for the flock. "A species every thirty years" is simple division and only works if the whole flock really is that young, which the film says with its "if". Counts of species run from 300 to 700.

  • Uncertain

    Nile perch was not the only cause

    The 40 and 65 percent are the same loss, about 200 species, divided by about 500 or by about 300. The perch was released in secret by fisheries officials in the mid-1950s, which makes "invited" a loose word, and trawling had already cut the cichlids from 1973. Murky, low-oxygen water from algal blooms added to it. Some species have recovered while the perch declined.

  • Simplified

    The Aztec canals

    Conservation International says the wetland system of islands and canals pre-dates the Aztecs; the chinampa farm islands are the part the Aztec era is known for. "Dug between farm islands since Aztec times" compresses that.

  • Uncertain

    Why the axolotl stays young

    Neoteny is well documented, and thyroid hormone can force metamorphosis at a cost. The idea that a calm, permanent lake gave it no reason to leave is a plausible hypothesis we found no primary source for. The film says so.

  • Uncertain

    "They're still in there"

    Water DNA shows axolotls are present, not how many. The 2025 survey caught none in nets, and the same press coverage notes the comparison to a reference library. The earlier density of 36 per square kilometre is itself an estimate.

  • Simplified

    Guam's snake and birds

    Sources say the snake arrived as a stowaway after the war, probably from the Admiralty Islands; the cargo detail comes from a single account and is not confirmed. Bird counts differ by source: USDA APHIS counts 10 of 13 native species lost, Wiles et al. 12 species likely gone, and many more reduced. The film says "most".

  • Simplified

    The moa

    The settlers' number and the 150 years are model estimates, not counts, and the comparison to a high school is ours. The film does not claim hunting alone did it: Holdaway et al. name hunting and removal of habitat.

  • Left out

    Stylisation, and what we left out

    The glowing ribbons of eDNA, the NO VISITORS sign and the locked door are drawings and metaphors. Left out: kakapo, the dodo, Komodo dragons (whether they are island giants is contested), Lake Malawi and Tanganyika, and rats as island predators.
